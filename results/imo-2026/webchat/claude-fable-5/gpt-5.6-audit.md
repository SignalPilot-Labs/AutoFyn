## Problem 1 — 7/7

### Termination

The proof uses the lexicographically ordered pair

$$
(P(C),c(C)),
$$

where $P(C)$ is the product of all board entries and $c(C)$ is the number of entries greater than one.

If $g=\gcd(m,n)$, the selected subproduct changes from $mn$ to

$$
g\cdot\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}g=\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)=\frac{mn}{g}.
$$

Thus $P$ falls strictly when $g>1$. When $g=1$, the pair becomes $(1,mn)$, so $P$ stays fixed and $c$ falls by exactly one. The written stabilization argument for $P$ is equivalent to lexicographic descent and correctly rules out an infinite play.

### Existence and uniqueness of the survivor

At a terminal board at most one entry is greater than one. The proof fixes a prime dividing an initial entry and observes that the corresponding exponent pair changes by

$$
(a,b)\longmapsto(\min(a,b),|a-b|).
$$

If at least one of $a,b$ is positive, at least one new exponent is positive, so that prime can never disappear from the board entirely. Hence the terminal board has exactly one non-unit $M$.

For every prime $p$, the ideal generated by all valuation coordinates,

$$
\sum_i v_p(c_i)\mathbb Z,
$$

is invariant, because

$$
a\mathbb Z+b\mathbb Z
=a\mathbb Z+(b-a)\mathbb Z
$$

after ordering $a\le b$. Equivalently, the gcd of all $p$-adic valuations is invariant. At the terminal board it equals $v_p(M)$, so

$$
v_p(M)=\gcd\bigl(v_p(a_1),\ldots,v_p(a_{2026})\bigr).
$$

This determines $M$ from the original board.

### Skeptical checks

- The termination argument covers both coprime and non-coprime moves.
- Zero valuation coordinates cause no difficulty in the subgroup/gcd invariant.
- The proof that at least one non-unit survives is independent of the later uniqueness invariant and is valid.
- Only finitely many primes divide the original product, so the displayed formula for $M$ is a finite product.

No mathematical defect was found. **Score: 7/7.**

$$
k=\left\lfloor\frac v\theta\right\rfloor+1

places $k\theta$ strictly inside the attainable interval $(v,v+w)$. One child contains $k\theta$ and the other contains $(n-k)\theta$. The $n=2$ case is separately and correctly handled by choosing $p=90^\circ$. Hence every starting triangle is won in finitely many cuts.

### Necessity

When $180^\circ/\theta$ is not an integer, call a positive multiple of $\theta$ below $180^\circ$ red. The three closure facts about red values—no complementary red pair, closure under sums below $180^\circ$, and closure under positive differences—are correct.

A fully green initial triangle exists because only finitely many values are forbidden. If a cut of a green triangle produced no all-green child, one red new angle could be selected from each child. The four possible selections force respectively:

- the parent angle $c$ to be a positive difference of red values;

Every possibility contradicts greenness. Shan-Yu can therefore keep a green child forever, so the angle $\theta$ never appears.

### Sufficiency and forced composition identity

$$

### Excluding fixed points

If $g(p)=0$ and $g(t)=e$ for $t\in[p,p+2e]$, the first squared inequality gives

$$
(t-p)^2\ge2e(t+p)+e^2.

$$

a contradiction. Hence the whole interval is fixed. Iterating from its right endpoint makes every point of $[p,\infty)$ fixed, contradicting the ray on which $g=e$. Therefore either $g\equiv0$ or $g\equiv e$, and all solutions are $f(x)=x+c$, $c\ge0$.
