## imo-2026-01

- Distinct openings:
  1. **(Assigned) Abstract-rewriting / confluence framing.** View board states as nodes of an ARS, moves as rewrite steps. Part (a) = termination, part (b) = uniqueness of normal form. Newman's Lemma (terminating + locally confluent ⟹ confluent ⟹ unique normal form) is the natural packaging.
  2. **(Found while probing, and now my top recommendation) Per-prime exponent invariant/monovariant framing.** For each prime p, track the vector of p-adic valuations (v_p(x_1),…,v_p(x_2026)) across the board. A move on entries m,n replaces the pair of exponents (a,b) at those two coordinates with (min(a,b), |a−b|) — because v_p(gcd(m,n)) = min(a,b) and v_p(lcm(m,n)/gcd(m,n)) = max(a,b) − min(a,b) = |a−b|. This holds **independently for every prime**, with the *same* choice of positions (i,j) shared across all primes since m,n are single integers.
  3. Global potential/monovariant framing: total product P = ∏(board entries) is non-increasing (new pair product = lcm(m,n) = mn/gcd(m,n) ≤ mn), strict decrease iff gcd(m,n) > 1; when gcd(m,n)=1 the pair becomes (1, mn), so the *count* of entries > 1 strictly drops instead. Combine into one lexicographic monovariant.

- Candidate technique(s): Newman's Lemma / local confluence (assigned lens) vs. direct invariant-plus-monovariant argument via Ω (number of prime factors with multiplicity) and per-prime gcd-of-exponents. **The direct route (opening 2+3) essentially subsumes and outperforms the confluence route** — see analysis below.

### Confluence-route analysis (as assigned)

- **Overlap/critical-pair cases** for two one-step moves from a common state, on pairs of positions {i,j} and {k,l}:
  - **Disjoint** ({i,j}∩{k,l}=∅): moves act on different coordinates, trivially commute — confluent with zero work.
  - **Share exactly one position** (e.g. j=k, so positions i,j,l are three distinct board entries x,y,z): this is the *only* nontrivial critical pair. It reduces to a 3-element sub-configuration where the two rewrites are "combine (x,y) first" vs "combine (y,z) first."
  - **Same pair** ({i,j}={k,l}): not a real divergence (same move).
- **Concrete test of the 3-way critical pair.** With (x,y,z) = (4,6,9): combining (x,y) first gives {2,6,9}; combining (y,z) first gives {4,3,6}. I ran both states forward (random move order, Python) many times: both terminate at the identical final multiset {1,1,6}, confirming M is unaffected — but the two intermediate states {2,6,9} and {3,4,6} are genuinely different multisets and do **not** meet at any intermediate common state (only at the final normal form). So "joinability" in the classical rewriting sense only occurs at the terminal state, i.e., local confluence in the strict ARS sense would have to be proved by tracking the *value* M, not the multiset shape, at each critical pair — which is exactly re-deriving the per-prime gcd invariant (opening 2) inside the critical-pair case. **Newman's Lemma buys no shortcut here**: verifying "local confluence" on the 3-entry critical pair requires the same core computation (gcd(min(a,b),|a−b|) = gcd(a,b), i.e. one step of the subtractive Euclidean algorithm preserves gcd) that directly proves the invariant. The confluence packaging adds formal ARS machinery (defining the state space, well-founded order, critical-pair enumeration over all trios, Newman's Lemma statement/citation) on top of the same arithmetic fact, with no reduction in the hard part.
- **Assessment: viable but not recommended as the lead approach.** It is correct and could be written up as a legitimate alternative, but it is strictly heavier than opening 2 for the same payoff. If pursued, the only real casework is the 3-entry overlap above (2 sub-cases up to symmetry: y between x,z in size or not — doesn't actually matter, the Euclidean-invariance argument is uniform), so it is *not* hiding hard casework, just redundant machinery.

### Recommended primary route (opening 2/3, found via probing — flag prominently to outliner)

- **Invariant (⟹ part b):** For each prime p, gcd(v_p(x_1),...,v_p(x_2026)) is preserved by every move, because gcd(min(a,b), |a−b|) = gcd(a,b) (elementary Euclidean-algorithm fact — a and b are the two exponents changed; gcd of the whole multiset factors through pairwise gcd since gcd(rest, a, b) = gcd(rest, gcd(a,b))). At the terminal state only M is >1, so v_p(M) equals that invariant gcd for every p. Hence M = ∏_p p^{gcd_i v_p(x_i)}, fully determined by the initial board, independent of the moves. This is a complete, direct proof of (b), no confluence needed.
- **Termination (⟹ part a):** use the lexicographic pair (Ω-total, #entries>1) where Ω-total = Σ Ω(x_i) (total count of prime factors with multiplicity across the board). Since v_p(m)+v_p(n) = v_p(gcd)+v_p(lcm/gcd) for every prime (as max+min = a+b), Ω-total decreases by exactly Ω(gcd(m,n)) each move, i.e. it strictly decreases whenever gcd(m,n) > 1, and stays fixed exactly when gcd(m,n)=1. In the gcd=1 case, the pair becomes (1, mn), so the count of entries >1 strictly drops by 1. Both components are bounded below (≥0), non-increasing, and can't both stay flat (Ω-total flat ⟹ #entries>1 drops), so the process terminates in finitely many moves at a state with at most one entry >1; it can't reach zero entries >1 since the product of survivors is preserved on the nose in total (only splits between two cells, never vanishes) — actually simplest: it stops when <2 entries are >1, which is the literal termination condition, so (a) just needs well-foundedness of this lexicographic order, which is standard.
- Both halves verified numerically (see below) on 5 random multisets with repeated random move orders — always converges to the exact predicted M.

- Cheap-kill candidates: the per-prime reduction to (a,b) ↦ (min(a,b), |a−b|) is itself the cheap structural kill — it decouples the whole problem into 2026-dimensional exponent vectors per prime, each governed by elementary Euclidean-algorithm identities. No heavier machinery (LTE, Zsigmondy, etc.) is needed.
