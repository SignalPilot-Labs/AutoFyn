## imo-2026-01

- Distinct openings:
  1. **Two-invariant potential-function route (recommended primary target).** Track the *total product* `P` of all 2026 board numbers. A move replaces `(m,n)` with `(gcd(m,n), lcm(m,n)/gcd(m,n))`; the new pair's product is `gcd(m,n)·lcm(m,n)/gcd(m,n) = lcm(m,n) = mn/gcd(m,n) ≤ mn`, with **equality iff gcd(m,n)=1**. So `P` is non-increasing, and strictly decreases (by a factor ≥2, since `gcd(m,n)≥2` in that case) whenever a non-coprime pair is chosen. Separately track `A` = number of board entries `>1`. Per move, `A` restricted to the touched pair can only stay the same (2→2, both new entries still >1) or drop (2→1 or 2→0); it can never increase, since only 2 slots are touched and a slot with value 1 can't be un-flipped to something bigger without another move on it, but even then the *max slots touched* is 2. Crucially: when `gcd(m,n)=1`, the new pair is `(1, mn)` — exactly one active entry survives, so `A` **strictly drops by 1** at every coprime move. So: `A` is non-increasing; whenever `A` stays constant, the move was non-coprime and `P` strictly drops by a factor ≥2. Since `A` starts ≤2026 and is a nonneg integer, and `P` starts finite and is a positive integer bounded below by 1, the lexicographic pair `(A,P)` (or just: at most 2026 "A-decreasing" moves, and at most `log2(P0)` "P-decreasing" moves) is well-founded ⟹ **finite termination**. This settles the "finitely many moves" half of part (a) directly, without needing the per-prime picture at all — worth flagging to the outliner as an independent, cleaner route to termination than a prime-by-prime argument.
  2. **Per-prime exponent-multiset reduction (the assigned lens).** Fix a prime `p`. Track the multiset of `v_p`-exponents across the 2026 positions. A board move on `(m,n)` sends `(v_p(m), v_p(n)) ↦ (min, max−min)` — this is exactly the two-element step of the subtractive Euclidean algorithm, and it's well known / easily reproved that `gcd(min, max−min) = gcd(v_p(m), v_p(n))`, so **the gcd of the whole exponent multiset for prime p is invariant across every move that touches two of its members** (trivial no-op if neither touched position has that prime). This gives the *candidate value* of `v_p(M)`: it must be `g_p := gcd` of the initial `v_p(a_i)` over all `i=1..2026` (with the convention `gcd(0,e)=e`, so `g_p` is just the gcd of the *nonzero* initial exponents at `p`; `g_p=0` iff no initial number is divisible by `p`). Numerically confirmed below.
  3. **Coupling caveat — the actual crux of part (a).** The per-prime games are NOT independent: a single board move applies to a chosen pair of *positions*, and updates **every** prime's exponent at those two positions simultaneously (using the same pair, same move-index). So one cannot just cite "each prime's subtractive game converges to one nonzero survivor" as 2026-many separate games running independently — the move sequence is shared. The real question for part (a) is why the process can't get stuck with, say, prime `p`'s exponents fully squashed to a single nonzero position `i`, and prime `q`'s exponents fully squashed to a *different* single nonzero position `j ≠ i`, leaving TWO board entries `>1` (position `i` divisible only by `p^{g_p}`, position `j` only by `q^{g_q}`) with **no available move** between them being impossible — but wait, both `i` and `j` are still `>1`, so a move IS available (any two entries `>1`, regardless of which primes they carry, can be chosen) — Confucius must keep moving until at most one entry is `>1`. So the real content isn't "can two positions survive" (impossible while the game runs, since >1 && >1 ⟹ movable) but rather (i) why does it *terminate* (opening 1 handles this), and (ii) why is the eventual configuration forced to look like each prime concentrating its full exponent-gcd onto whichever single position survives overall, and that same position must simultaneously host ALL primes' surviving exponents. This "simultaneous concentration onto one shared position" is the fact that needs proof, and it's exactly what's captured by tracking `M = Π_p p^{g_p}` as a candidate and showing directly `∏(board) / M` type invariants, or by an induction on number of moves showing the surviving nonzero entries' product of prime-power pieces reassembles into one number. Flag to outliner: don't only argue prime-by-prime independently; must also justify why the *same* index ends up carrying every prime's residual gcd exponent (or equivalently, prove `M` divides / is divisible in a way pinning it to a single position) — likely via a stronger invariant than the naive per-prime gcd (e.g., relate to gcd of the *entire number* board members after normalizing, or an inductive argument on board size reducing 2 numbers to 1 "logical slot" at a time).
  4. **Small-case / two-number sanity check as scaffolding.** With exactly 2 numbers `m,n`, the process is literally the subtractive Euclidean algorithm on the pair itself (since there's only one pair to ever pick), terminating at `(gcd(m,n)·d, 1)`-ish states... actually simulated below: `{4,8} → {4,2} → {2,2} → {2,1}`, final `M=2`. Compare with `gcd(4,8)=4` — **M is NOT `gcd(a_i)`**, it's the finer "coordinatewise-gcd-of-exponents" quantity. This is an important early check the outliner should verify explicitly (a natural wrong guess "M=gcd of the numbers" fails and should be flagged as a trap).

  - Active-count `A` (# entries `>1`) is non-increasing and strictly drops on every coprime move — cheap structural bound, bounds total move count crudely by `2026 + log2(P0)`.
  - `gcd(min,max−min) = gcd(a,b)` is a one-line algebraic identity (standard Euclidean-algorithm fact) — immediately gives the per-prime invariant `g_p`, hence the *value* `M = Π p^{g_p}` as a natural conjecture to prove exists and is attained.

- Small-case / intuition notes (numerically verified via Python `sympy`/`math.gcd` simulation, `/tmp/sim.py`, `/tmp/sim2.py` — this is evidence/conjecture-strength, not proof):
  - **Single-prime exponent game** (treating one prime's exponent multiset in isolation, moves = pick any two positions with `>0` exponent, apply `(a,b)→(min,max−min)` until at most one nonzero remains): over 20 random trials with multiset sizes 3–8 and random initial exponents 0–10, **always** terminated with exactly one nonzero survivor, and that survivor's value **always** equals the gcd of the initial (nonzero) exponents. Consistent with the standard fact that the subtractive Euclidean algorithm on a multiset concentrates onto the gcd.
  - **Full board simulation** (real integers 2–60, sizes 3–7, `sympy.factorint`, random legal move order each trial, 15 trials): predicted `M = Π_p p^{gcd_i v_p(a_i)}` matched the actual simulated terminal survivor in **every** trial (asserted and passed, no failures), and in every trial exactly one entry `>1` remained. Strong numerical confirmation of both part (a) (uniqueness of survivor) and part (b) (order-independence, since random move orders always hit the same predicted `M`) and pins the **exact formula** for `M`: `M = Π over primes p of p^{gcd of the p-adic valuations of the 2026 initial numbers}` (equivalently: for the largest exponent set, replace zero valuations by "absent" — gcd treats them as identity).
  - Two-number sanity check: `{4,8}` (i.e. `2^2, 2^3`) → simulated path `{4,8}→{4,2}→{2,2}→{2,1}`, final `M=2 = 2^{gcd(2,3)}`. Confirms `M ≠ gcd(a,b)` in general (here `gcd(4,8)=4≠2`), and matches the exponent-gcd formula exactly.
  - The formula `M = Π p^{gcd_i v_p(a_i)}` is reminiscent of (but distinct from) `gcd(a_1,...,a_n) = Π p^{min_i v_p(a_i)}` — the game computes a **gcd-of-exponents**, one level up from the usual min-of-exponents gcd. This "second-order gcd" structure (gcd applied to the exponent vector rather than min applied to it) is the punchline the outliner should aim the proof at, and is worth stating explicitly as the target formula for `M` in the outline (even though `task: proof_only` / `answer_type: none` per problems.jsonl means no numeric answer needs to be output — but stating M's closed form makes part (b)'s invariance argument concrete and checkable).
