### Distinct openings (prime-support / admissibility lens)

1. **a₁-anchor lemma (the single strongest and cheapest structural fact I found).**
   The admissibility condition requires, for *every* n, `gcd(a_{n+1}, a_i) > 1` for **every** `i = 1,...,n` — in particular always for `i=1`. So **every term a_m (m ≥ 2) shares a prime factor with a_1**. Since `a_1` has only finitely many prime factors P_1 = primes(a_1) (a fixed finite set, known from the start), this means: *the entire sequence, from a_2 onward, is contained in the union of arithmetic progressions {multiples of p : p ∈ P_1}.* I verified this numerically on every example I built (a_1=15 → P_1={3,5}, every term divisible by 3 or 5; a_1=35 → P_1={5,7}, every term divisible by 5 or 7; a_1 prime → every term divisible by that single prime). This is an easy, fully rigorous lemma (one line: gcd(a_{n+1},a_1)>1 ⟹ they share a prime; a_1's prime factors are fixed) and should be lemma #1 in any outline.

2. **Generalized anchor for every fixed index i, not just i=1.**
   By the same argument applied with i fixed and n→∞: for every fixed i, all a_n with n>i are divisible by some prime of P_i := primes(a_i). This is a whole *family* of anchor constraints, one per index, all of which a_{n+1} must satisfy *simultaneously* (hit P_1 AND P_2 AND ... AND P_n). This is a genuine "hitting set / set-cover" structure on the primes of a_{n+1}. The natural big question this opening raises: does the union ⋃_i P_i ever stop growing (only finitely many *new* primes get "recruited" across the whole infinite sequence)? Numerically the recruited set does stabilize (e.g. a_1=35 with P_1={5,7} eventually also uses 2 and 3, reaching a final active set {2,3,5,7} with L=210=2·3·5·7), but *why* it stabilizes (rather than an infinite chain of ever-rarer new primes being pulled in one by one) is the hard, unproven core of the problem. This is the natural place to look for the "why does it terminate" argument — e.g. a growth-rate vs density argument, or a minimal-counterexample / extremal argument on the smallest newly-recruited prime.

3. **Pigeonhole on the prime factorization of a_{n+1} itself (crux-inspired).**
   For fixed n, a_{n+1} needs a witnessing prime for each i=1,...,n: a map φ: {1,...,n} → primes(a_{n+1}), φ(i) = some prime dividing gcd(a_{n+1}, a_i). Since ω(a_{n+1}) (number of distinct prime factors) is only O(log a_{n+1} / log log a_{n+1}), and if the sequence grows only linearly in n (which the target conclusion actually forces: a_{n+T}=a_n+L implies a_n = Θ(n) asymptotically), then ω(a_{n+1}) = O(log n). By pigeonhole, some single prime p | a_{n+1} must satisfy p | a_i for at least n/ω(a_{n+1}) = n / O(log n) of the indices i — a positive-density (indeed near-full-density) subset of all earlier terms share one prime with the new term. This is a genuinely useful "many earlier terms share a prime with the new term" fact, but it presupposes the O(n) growth rate, which is circular unless proved independently first (see cheap-kill idea below for breaking that circularity).

4. **Reformulate as a covering-congruence problem.**
   Once (if) the active prime set S = {p_1,...,p_k} stabilizes, "smallest integer m > a_n admissible" becomes a purely modular question: m is admissible iff, working mod L=∏p_i^{e_i} (or the relevant lcm), m's residue is compatible with the residues of a *bounded window* of recent terms (older terms become redundant once "covered" by a newer term using the same prime). This is structurally the same flavor as **Erdős covering congruence systems** and **Jacobsthal's function** g(P) (the max gap between consecutive integers divisible by some prime in a fixed finite set P) — I didn't find either named in `knowledge_base.md` (no "covering" / "Jacobsthal" entries), so this connection is not pre-loaded; it would need to be built and proven from scratch (CLAUDE.md forbids citing corpus/KB facts without reproof anyway). This framing is the natural bridge from "prime-support" to the actual periodicity conclusion T,L: L should end up being (a multiple of) the primorial-type product built from S, and T the count of admissible residues mod L in one period — but proving that the *pattern of admissible residues itself* becomes periodic (not just "some prime of S divides everything") is a second nontrivial gap beyond the finiteness of S.

### Cheap-kill candidates
- **Anchor-lemma pruning (opening 1)**: immediately restricts every a_n (n≥2) to lie in a union of |P_1| ≤ Ω(a_1) arithmetic progressions. Cheap, rigorous, and should be adopted as a shared first lemma regardless of which framing wins — it costs one line and kills the "the sequence could use arbitrarily many unrelated primes with no common anchor" worry outright.
- **Parity/size check on Ω(a_{n+1})**: to break the circularity in opening 3 (needing linear growth to bound ω(a_{n+1}) but wanting to use ω(a_{n+1}) to prove linear growth), note a weaker a-priori bound suffices to start: `a_{n+1} < a_n + (something explicit, e.g. a_1-dependent constant)` might be provable directly and independently from a greedy density argument (there is always an admissible candidate within a bounded window of a_n because multiples of any single p∈P_1 recur with gap ≤ p_max ∈ P_1, and such a multiple of p (once large enough) automatically has gcd>1 with every earlier a_i that is itself a multiple of some prime, PROVIDED it's simultaneously checked against the *other* P_i-type constraints) — this bounded-gap-existence argument (not fully checked, flagged as a lemma to attempt, not asserted) would break the circularity in opening 3 without needing the periodicity conclusion itself.

### Analogous past problems (cruxes)
- **aimo-0447** (number_theory / divisibility-and-gcd) — statement: "gcd(a+i, b+j) > 1 for all i,j ∈ {0,...,n} ⟹ min{a,b} > (cn)^{n/2}." Crux move: **assign to each cell (i,j) of the covering grid a witnessing prime dividing the gcd, turning the "gcd > 1 everywhere" hypothesis into a prime-covering of a grid**, then pigeonhole/count how many cells one prime can occupy (≤⌈N/p⌉² by "prime p divides ≤⌈N/p⌉ terms of an interval of length N"), forcing most cells to carry large distinct primes, which forces a big product bound. This is the closest genuine analogue I found: our problem's admissibility condition ("gcd(a_{n+1},a_i)>1 for every i≤n") is structurally the *same* "gcd>1 across a whole row/set" hypothesis as aimo-0447's grid condition. The witnessing-prime-assignment + pigeonhole-on-small-primes-vs-large-primes technique from aimo-0447 is directly adaptable to opening 3 above (bounding indices serviced by one prime of a_{n+1}) and possibly to opening 2 (bounding how many *distinct* primes ever need to be recruited, by an analogous "small primes cover almost everything, so only few large/new primes are ever needed" density count).

Numerical check refutes this for a_1=35=5·7 (final active set is {2,3,5,7}, strictly larger than P_1={5,7}) and for a_1=105=3·5·7 (also recruits 2, final L=210). ### Small-case / intuition notes (all conjectural, verified only on finitely many numeric instances)
Built the greedy sequence with brute-force gcd search in Python for many starting values a_1 (2–2000+ terms each). Findings, all **conjectural** (small-case evidence, not proof):
- If a_1 = p is prime: the sequence is exactly p, 2p, 3p, 4p, ... (T=1, L=p) — clean, and this case is fully understood/provable directly: since P_1={p} is a single prime, every term is divisible by p, and then the smallest admissible next term is trivially the next multiple of p (its gcd with everything is automatically ≥ p). Good base case / sanity check for any general lemma.
- If a_1 = p·q (two odd primes) the picture bifurcates sharply depending on which prime "wins": a_1=21=3·7 and a_1=63=9·7 both collapse immediately to T=1, L=3 (the denser prime 3 completely takes over, 7 becomes irrelevant after step 1). But a_1=15=3·5 does NOT collapse to L=3; it settles into T=8, L=30 — because occasional multiples of 5 that are also even (hence compatible with an even earlier term) sneak in earlier than the next multiple of 3, meaning 2 gets recruited as an active prime and the final period genuinely uses all of {2,3,5}. This shows the eventual (T, L) is a delicate function of the actual numeric race between primes, not just which primes divide a_1 — genuine combinatorial content, not just an anchor-lemma corollary.
- a_1=35=5·7 → T=34, L=210=2·3·5·7 (all four small primes get recruited even though only 5,7 divide a_1).
- a_1=105=3·5·7 → T=58, L=210 (same final L as a_1=35, different T — suggests L might depend only on which primes ever get "recruited," while T depends on the finer residue-pattern/history).
- a_1=77=7·11 → T=18, L=154=2·7·11 (only recruits 2, not 3 or 5 — so recruitment is not "always pulls in all small primes," it depends on specific gaps).
- a_1=91=7·13 → T=20, L=182=2·7·13.
- a_1=143=11·13 → T=64, L=858=2·3·11·13 (here both 2 and 3 get recruited).
This pattern (some starts recruit only one extra small prime, others recruit two, base cases with a very dense prime factor like 3 collapse instantly) is exactly the numerical fingerprint of the hard, unresolved part of the problem: characterizing *which* new primes get recruited and *why the recruitment process terminates* rather than continuing to pull in ever-larger primes forever. That termination argument is the crux gap the outliner needs to target; I did not find a way to shortcut it, only to confirm (numerically, on ~12 cases up to a_1=143) that termination does empirically occur every time.
