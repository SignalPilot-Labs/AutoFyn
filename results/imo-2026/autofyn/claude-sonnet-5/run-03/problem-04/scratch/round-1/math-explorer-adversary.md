## imo-2026-04

### Setup reduction (needed context for all findings below)
A cut is purely angle-combinatorial: if the current triangle has angles (α,β,γ) (α+β+γ=180) and Mulan cuts from the apex opposite the side carrying α (i.e. splits α into t, α−t, t ∈ (0,α) freely chosen — she has full continuous control), the two children are
- T1 = {β, t, 180−β−t}
- T2 = {γ, α−t, β+t}.
Key structural fact: the two "new" angles at the cut point, 180−β−t (in T1) and β+t (in T2), are **supplementary** (sum to 180) for every choice of apex and t — they're literally the two angles on either side of P on line through the base. This single fact drives both the attack and the defense.

### Distinct openings (defender's lens)
1. **θ > 90° ⇒ Shan-Yu wins (proved, not just conjectured).** Since the two new "danger" angles at P sum to 180 and θ>90 gives 180−θ<θ, they can never both be ≥θ simultaneously (2θ>180). So at least one child always has its danger angle <θ. Shan-Yu's strategy: pick any initial triangle with all angles <θ (e.g. equilateral 60-60-60, valid since θ>90>60), then always discard whichever child's *new* angle is ≥θ and keep the other. Induction: if all three current angles are <θ, the kept child inherits two old angles (<θ, invariant) plus t or α−t (<α<θ automatically since t<α) plus the safe danger angle (<θ by the pigeonhole above). So "all angles <θ" is an invariant Shan-Yu can maintain forever ⇒ Mulan never wins. I verified this with a 200,000-trial randomized adversarial simulation (adversary picks θ, initial triangle, and cut points to try to break the invariant) — zero failures. Code path: `/tmp/sim.py`.
2. **θ = 90° ⇒ Mulan wins in ≤1 move, always.** The altitude from any vertex whose two OTHER angles are both acute creates two right angles at the foot (supplementary danger angles both =90 exactly, since 180−θ=θ only at θ=90). Any triangle has at most one angle ≥90°, so such a vertex always exists (already won if triangle is already right). This is the unique value where the "supplementary pair" argument produces exact equality on BOTH sides for free, from any starting triangle — matches algebraically: solving the 4 sub-case equations for "both children contain θ in one move" gives only two solutions: apex angle = 2θ (needs a pre-existing 2θ angle), or θ=90 exactly (universal, no precondition). This means θ=90 is a genuine boundary/threshold, not just a convenient example.
3. **Doubling/halving chain from 90°: θ = 90/2^k wins for every k≥0.** From (2), produce a 90° angle in both children (1 move, universal). Then bisect that 90° angle exactly (mechanism "apex=2θ ⇒ split t=θ": since apex=90=2·45, splitting evenly gives both children a 45° angle). Iterate: split 45° angle in half ⇒ 22.5°, etc. Each step is a guaranteed "both children get the target" move once the previous target is present, so this yields an explicit finite winning line for every dyadic-in-90 value. This does NOT by itself cover all θ≤90 — it's a sufficient sub-family, and the general θ<90 case needs a genuinely different/deeper forcing argument (probably the actual crux of the "hard" direction), which I did not attempt to complete (out of scope for this lens).
4. **General θ<90 has no analogous clean invariant defense** — I checked whether Shan-Yu could maintain "all angles >θ" (the mirror invariant to (1)) and it fails: for θ<90, 180−θ>90>θ, so it is NOT guaranteed that at least one danger angle stays >θ; in fact both can simultaneously be forced ≥θ (only exact equality needs a further condition, but the safety margin the θ>90 proof relies on is simply absent). Empirically, even *non-adversarial random* cuts already break the naive "stay <θ / >θ" tracking a nontrivial fraction of the time when θ<90 (`/tmp/sim2.py`, ~2% failure rate on purely random play, no exploitation). This supports (but does not prove) that Mulan should win for all 0<θ≤90 rather than only the dyadic values found in (3).

### Candidate technique(s)
