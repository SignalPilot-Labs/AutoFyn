Technique: **Direct/constructive characterization proof**, in two directions
(necessity via a forced functional equation + a monotone-defect/telescoping
squeeze; sufficiency via a direct algebraic identity reducing to the classical
QM-AM-GM inequality). The spine of the necessity direction is: (i) a
forced-equality substitution to extract an exact functional equation (already
done, see Current best facts 3–4), then (ii) a **second, different**
substitution into the inequality pair that yields a *quadratic-defect
inequality* bounding `d(x)-d(y) := (f(x)-x)-(f(y)-y)` by `O((y-x)^2)`, then
(iii) a **telescoping/Archimedean squeeze** (partition `[a,b]` into `N` pieces,
sum the local bound, let `N→∞`) that forces `d` to be globally constant. This
last step is the "cheap kill" for the whole gap: it avoids any
continuity/monotonicity/Cauchy-equation machinery — it is pure algebra plus the
Archimedean property of `R`.

Notation: write the two inequalities from the problem as
- **(QM-ineq)**: `2(x^2 + f(y)^2) ≥ (f(x)+y)^2` for all `x,y ∈ R_{>0}` (squares
  of the left `≥` middle part of the chain; valid since both sides are
  manifestly `≥ 0`),
- **(GM-ineq)**: `(f(x)+y)^2 ≥ 4x f(y)` for all `x,y ∈ R_{>0}` (squares of the
  middle `≥` right part; valid since `f(x)+y > 0`).

and `d(t) := f(t) - t` for `t ∈ R_{>0}`.

## Full proof

**Claim.** A function $f : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ satisfies
$$
\sqrt{\frac{x^2+f(y)^2}{2}} \;\ge\; \frac{f(x)+y}{2} \;\ge\; \sqrt{x f(y)} \qquad \text{for all } x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0} \tag{$\ast$}
$$
if and only if there is a constant $c \ge 0$ such that $f(x) = x+c$ for all $x \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

Throughout, $d(t) := f(t) - t$ for $t \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ (so $f(t) = t + d(t)$).

We repeatedly use the elementary fact that for real numbers $u,v \ge 0$,
$$
u \ge v \iff u^2 \ge v^2, \tag{Sq}
$$
since $t \mapsto t^2$ is strictly increasing on $[0,\infty)$.

---

### Part 1: Necessity — every solution has the form $f(x)=x+c$, $c\ge 0$

Assume $f$ satisfies $(\ast)$ for all $x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

#### Step 1. The functional equation $f(f(y)) = 2f(y)-y$.

Fix $y_0 \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ arbitrary and substitute $x := f(y_0)$ into $(\ast)$ evaluated
at $(x,y) = (f(y_0), y_0)$. This substitution is legitimate because $f(y_0) \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$
(as $f$ maps $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ into $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$), so $x = f(y_0)$ is a valid choice of the
free variable $x$ in $(\ast)$. With this choice, $(\ast)$ reads
$$
\sqrt{\frac{f(y_0)^2+f(y_0)^2}{2}} \;\ge\; \frac{f(f(y_0))+y_0}{2} \;\ge\; \sqrt{f(y_0)\cdot f(y_0)}.
$$
Both outer expressions are, by direct computation, equal to $f(y_0)$:
$$
\sqrt{\frac{f(y_0)^2+f(y_0)^2}{2}} = \sqrt{f(y_0)^2} = f(y_0), \qquad \sqrt{f(y_0)\cdot f(y_0)} = \sqrt{f(y_0)^2} = f(y_0),
$$
using $f(y_0) > 0$ so the square root is the positive value $f(y_0)$ itself. Hence the chain
collapses to
$$
f(y_0) \;\ge\; \frac{f(f(y_0))+y_0}{2} \;\ge\; f(y_0),
$$
which forces the middle term to equal both ends:
$$
\frac{f(f(y_0))+y_0}{2} = f(y_0) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(f(y_0)) = 2f(y_0) - y_0.
$$
Since $y_0 \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ was arbitrary, we have proved
$$
f(f(y)) = 2f(y) - y \qquad \text{for all } y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}. \tag{FE}
$$
(This is a direct computation forced by an equality chain at a single specific value of $x$;
it is not a disguised general "equality case of QM $=$ GM implies the two arguments are equal"
argument, and needs no such general lemma.)

#### Step 2. $f(x) \ge x$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

Fix $y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ and define a sequence $(y_n)_{n \ge 0}$ by
$$
y_0 := y, \qquad y_{n+1} := f(y_n) \quad (n \ge 0).
$$
Since $f : \mathbb{R}_{>0} \to \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ and $y_0 = y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$, induction on $n$
shows $y_n \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ is well-defined for every $n \ge 0$ (if $y_n>0$ then
$y_{n+1}=f(y_n)>0$ since $f$ takes values in $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$).

**Sub-claim: $y_n = y + n\,d(y)$ for all $n \ge 0$.**

First we show the increments $e_n := y_{n+1}-y_n$ are all equal to $d(y) = f(y)-y$, by
induction on $n$.

- *Base case* $n=0$: $e_0 = y_1 - y_0 = f(y) - y = d(y)$.
- *Inductive step*: suppose $e_n = d(y)$. Apply (FE) at the point $y_n$ (valid, since $y_n
  \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$): $f(f(y_n)) = 2f(y_n) - y_n$, i.e. $f(y_{n+1}) = 2y_{n+1} - y_n$, i.e.
  $y_{n+2} = 2y_{n+1}-y_n$. Hence
  $$
  e_{n+1} = y_{n+2}-y_{n+1} = (2y_{n+1}-y_n) - y_{n+1} = y_{n+1}-y_n = e_n = d(y).
  $$

So $e_n = d(y)$ for every $n \ge 0$. Now induct again to get $y_n = y+n\,d(y)$: the base case
$n=0$ is $y_0=y$, true; and if $y_n = y+n\,d(y)$ then $y_{n+1} = y_n+e_n = y+n\,d(y)+d(y) =
y+(n+1)d(y)$. This proves the sub-claim.

**Conclusion.** Suppose, for contradiction, that $d(y) < 0$ for some $y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$.
Since $d(y)<0$, the sequence $y_n = y+n\,d(y) \to -\infty$ as $n\to\infty$; concretely, choosing
any integer $N > \dfrac{y}{-d(y)} = \dfrac{-y}{d(y)}$ (possible by the Archimedean property of
$\mathbb{R}$, as $-y/d(y)$ is a fixed positive real number) gives
$$
y_N = y + N\,d(y) < y + \frac{-y}{d(y)}\cdot d(y) = y - y = 0,
$$
(multiplying the inequality $N > -y/d(y)$ through by $d(y)$, which is negative, reverses the
direction: $N\,d(y) < \left(\frac{-y}{d(y)}\right) d(y) = -y$, i.e. $y+N\,d(y) < 0$). But
$y_N \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ by construction, so $y_N > 0$ — a contradiction.
Hence $d(y) \ge 0$, i.e.
$$
f(y) \ge y \qquad \text{for all } y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}. \tag{Step 2}
$$

#### Step 3. Squared forms of $(\ast)$.

Both parts of $(\ast)$ have manifestly nonnegative sides (all terms are square roots of
positive numbers, or of the form $(f(x)+y)/2$ with $f(x),y>0$), so by (Sq) we may square each
inequality of $(\ast)$ separately without changing its validity or direction:

- squaring $\sqrt{(x^2+f(y)^2)/2} \ge (f(x)+y)/2$ gives
  $$
  2\big(x^2+f(y)^2\big) \ge (f(x)+y)^2 \qquad \text{for all } x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}; \tag{QM-ineq}
  $$
- squaring $(f(x)+y)/2 \ge \sqrt{x f(y)}$ gives
  $$
  (f(x)+y)^2 \ge 4x f(y) \qquad \text{for all } x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}. \tag{GM-ineq}
  $$

(QM-ineq) will not be needed again after this point — the rest of the necessity argument uses
only (GM-ineq) together with (FE) and Step 2.

#### Step 4. The quadratic-defect inequality (E).

**Claim (E).** For all $x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$,
$$
4f(x)\big(d(x)-d(y)\big) + (x-y)^2 \;\ge\; 0.
$$

**Proof.** In (GM-ineq), substitute the free variable $x$ by $f(x)$ (legitimate, since $f(x) \in
\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ whenever $x \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$): (GM-ineq) applied at the point $(f(x), y)$
reads
$$
\big(f(f(x))+y\big)^2 \;\ge\; 4 f(x) f(y).
$$
By (FE) (applied at $y_0 = x$), $f(f(x)) = 2f(x)-x$. Substituting this in:
$$
\big(2f(x)-x+y\big)^2 \;\ge\; 4 f(x) f(y). \tag{4.1}
$$
Now write $f(x) = x+d(x)$, $f(y) = y+d(y)$ and expand both sides of (4.1) in terms of $x,y,d(x),d(y)$.

First,
$$
2f(x)-x+y = 2(x+d(x)) - x + y = x+y+2d(x).
$$
So
$$
\big(2f(x)-x+y\big)^2 = \big(x+y+2d(x)\big)^2 = (x+y)^2 + 4d(x)(x+y) + 4d(x)^2.
$$
Also
$$
4f(x)f(y) = 4(x+d(x))(y+d(y)) = 4xy + 4x\,d(y) + 4y\,d(x) + 4\,d(x)d(y).
$$
Subtracting,
$$
\big(2f(x)-x+y\big)^2 - 4f(x)f(y) = (x+y)^2 - 4xy + 4d(x)(x+y) - 4x\,d(y) - 4y\,d(x) + 4d(x)^2 - 4d(x)d(y).
$$
Now $(x+y)^2-4xy = x^2-2xy+y^2 = (x-y)^2$, and expanding $4d(x)(x+y) = 4x\,d(x)+4y\,d(x)$, the
terms $4y\,d(x)$ and $-4y\,d(x)$ cancel. So this simplifies to
$$
\big(2f(x)-x+y\big)^2 - 4f(x)f(y) = (x-y)^2 + 4x\,d(x) + 4d(x)^2 - 4x\,d(y) - 4d(x)d(y).
$$
Group the last four terms as
$$
4x\,d(x) + 4d(x)^2 - 4x\,d(y) - 4d(x)d(y) = 4d(x)\big(x+d(x)\big) - 4d(y)\big(x+d(x)\big) = 4\big(x+d(x)\big)\big(d(x)-d(y)\big) = 4f(x)\big(d(x)-d(y)\big),
$$
using $x+d(x)=f(x)$. Hence we have derived the **exact polynomial identity**
$$
\big(2f(x)-x+y\big)^2 - 4f(x)f(y) \;=\; (x-y)^2 + 4f(x)\big(d(x)-d(y)\big) \qquad \text{for all } x,y\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}. \tag{4.2}
$$
(This identity was also checked symbolically: expanding both sides as polynomials in
$x,y,d(x),d(y)$ and subtracting gives identically $0$.)

Combining (4.1) and (4.2): the left side of (4.2) is $\ge 0$ by (4.1), so the right side of
(4.2) is $\ge 0$ too, i.e.
$$
4f(x)\big(d(x)-d(y)\big) + (x-y)^2 \;\ge\; 0 \qquad \text{for all } x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}, \tag{E}
$$
which is exactly the claimed inequality (E). $\blacksquare$ (Step 4)

Note that (E) was derived using only (GM-ineq) and (FE); it does not use Step 2, injectivity,
or any other fact.

#### Step 5. $d$ is a global constant.

**Claim.** $d(p) = d(q)$ for all $p,q \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

Since $d(p)=d(p)$ trivially when $p=q$, it suffices to prove $d(a) = d(b)$ for arbitrary
$a,b \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ with $a<b$ (for general $p \ne q$, apply this with $\{a,b\} = \{\min(p,q),\max(p,q)\}$).

Fix such $a<b$. For each positive integer $N$, set $\Delta := (b-a)/N > 0$ and
$$
x_i := a + i\Delta, \qquad i=0,1,\dots,N,
$$
so $x_0 = a$, $x_N=b$, $x_{i+1}-x_i=\Delta$ for each $i$, and every $x_i \in [a,b] \subset
\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ (in particular $x_i \ge a$).

**(a) Upper bound on $d(b)-d(a)$.** For each $i=0,\dots,N-1$, apply (E) at $(x,y)=(x_i,x_{i+1})$:
$$
4f(x_i)\big(d(x_i)-d(x_{i+1})\big) + (x_i-x_{i+1})^2 \ge 0.
$$
Since $(x_i-x_{i+1})^2 = \Delta^2$, rearranging gives
$$
d(x_{i+1}) - d(x_i) \;\le\; \frac{\Delta^2}{4f(x_i)}.
$$
By Step 2, $f(x_i) \ge x_i \ge a$, so $\dfrac{\Delta^2}{4f(x_i)} \le \dfrac{\Delta^2}{4a}$. Hence
$$
d(x_{i+1})-d(x_i) \;\le\; \frac{\Delta^2}{4a} \qquad \text{for } i=0,\dots,N-1.
$$
Summing telescopically over $i=0,\dots,N-1$:
$$
d(b)-d(a) = d(x_N)-d(x_0) = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\big(d(x_{i+1})-d(x_i)\big) \;\le\; N\cdot \frac{\Delta^2}{4a} = \frac{(b-a)^2}{4aN}. \tag{5.1}
$$

**(b) Upper bound on $d(a)-d(b)$.** Symmetrically, for each $i=0,\dots,N-1$, apply (E) at
$(x,y)=(x_{i+1},x_i)$:
$$
4f(x_{i+1})\big(d(x_{i+1})-d(x_i)\big) + (x_{i+1}-x_i)^2 \ge 0,
$$
and again $(x_{i+1}-x_i)^2=\Delta^2$, so
$$
d(x_i) - d(x_{i+1}) \;\le\; \frac{\Delta^2}{4f(x_{i+1})} \;\le\; \frac{\Delta^2}{4a},
$$
using Step 2 again ($f(x_{i+1}) \ge x_{i+1} \ge a$). Summing telescopically:
$$
d(a)-d(b) = \sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\big(d(x_i)-d(x_{i+1})\big) \;\le\; \frac{(b-a)^2}{4aN}. \tag{5.2}
$$

**(c) Passing to the limit.** Inequalities (5.1) and (5.2) hold for *every* positive integer
$N$; the right-hand side $K/N$ (with $K := (b-a)^2/(4a)$, a fixed positive real number
independent of $N$) can be made smaller than any prescribed $\varepsilon>0$ by the Archimedean
property of $\mathbb{R}$ (choose $N > K/\varepsilon$).

Suppose, for contradiction, that $L := d(b)-d(a) > 0$. Applying the Archimedean property with
$\varepsilon = L$, choose an integer $N > K/L$; then $K/N < L$. But (5.1) gives $L \le K/N$, so
$L \le K/N < L$, a contradiction. Hence $d(b)-d(a) \le 0$. The identical argument applied to
(5.2) shows $d(a)-d(b) \le 0$. Combining the two, $d(b)-d(a) = 0$, i.e. $d(a)=d(b)$.

Since $a<b$ were arbitrary points of $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, this proves $d(a)=d(b)$ for all
$a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}$: **$d$ is constant on $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$.** Write $d(t) \equiv c$ for a
constant $c \in \mathbb{R}$, so $f(t) = t+c$ for all $t \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$. By Step 2,
$c = d(t) \ge 0$ for any $t$, so $c \ge 0$.

This completes the proof that every solution of $(\ast)$ has the form $f(x)=x+c$ for some
constant $c \ge 0$.

---

### Part 2: Sufficiency — every $f(x)=x+c$ with $c \ge 0$ is a solution

Fix a constant $c \ge 0$ and define $f(x) := x+c$ for $x \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$.

**$f$ maps $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$ into $\mathbb{R}_{>0}$.** For $x>0$ and $c \ge 0$, $f(x)=x+c \ge x
> 0$, so $f(x) \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$. (Conversely, if $c<0$ were allowed, taking any $x \in
(0,-c) \subset \mathbb{R}_{>0}$ — nonempty since $-c>0$ — would give $f(x) = x+c < 0 \notin
\mathbb{R}_{>0}$; so $c \ge 0$ is exactly what is needed, and no more, for $f$ to be a
well-defined function $\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}$, consistent with Part 1's conclusion.)

**Key identity.** For all $x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$,
$$
f(x)+y = (x+c)+y = x+(y+c) = x+f(y). \tag{5.3}
$$
So the middle term of $(\ast)$ satisfies
$$
\frac{f(x)+y}{2} \overset{(5.3)}{=} \frac{x+f(y)}{2},
$$
i.e. it equals *exactly* the arithmetic mean of $x$ and $f(y)$. Hence, for this $f$, the
required chain $(\ast)$ becomes
$$
\sqrt{\frac{x^2+f(y)^2}{2}} \;\ge\; \frac{x+f(y)}{2} \;\ge\; \sqrt{x f(y)}, \tag{5.4}
$$
which is precisely the classical **QM $\ge$ AM $\ge$ GM inequality** for the two positive
real numbers $x$ and $f(y) = y+c$ (positive since $y>0$, $c\ge0$) — this is the standard
inequality named in `knowledge_base.md` ("Standard inequalities: AM-GM, Cauchy-Schwarz, QM-AM,
Schur. Equality cases pin down the extremal configuration"). For completeness we verify (5.4)
directly for arbitrary $a,b \ge 0$ (with $a=x$, $b=f(y)$):

- **QM $\ge$ AM.** We must show $\sqrt{(a^2+b^2)/2} \ge (a+b)/2$. Both sides are $\ge 0$, so by
  (Sq) this is equivalent to $(a^2+b^2)/2 \ge (a+b)^2/4$, i.e. $2(a^2+b^2) \ge (a+b)^2$, i.e.
  $$
  2(a^2+b^2) - (a+b)^2 = 2a^2+2b^2-a^2-2ab-b^2 = a^2-2ab+b^2 = (a-b)^2 \ge 0,
  $$
  which holds for all real $a,b$. Hence QM $\ge$ AM.
- **AM $\ge$ GM.** We must show $(a+b)/2 \ge \sqrt{ab}$ (note $ab \ge 0$ since $a,b\ge0$, so
  $\sqrt{ab}$ is a well-defined nonnegative real). Both sides are $\ge 0$, so by (Sq) this is
  equivalent to $(a+b)^2/4 \ge ab$, i.e. $(a+b)^2 \ge 4ab$, i.e.
  $$
  (a+b)^2 - 4ab = a^2+2ab+b^2-4ab = a^2-2ab+b^2 = (a-b)^2 \ge 0,
  $$
  which holds for all real $a,b$. Hence AM $\ge$ GM.

Applying these two verified inequalities with $a=x>0$, $b=f(y)=y+c>0$ proves (5.4), hence
$(\ast)$, for every $x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$. So every $f(x)=x+c$ with $c \ge 0$ is indeed a
solution of $(\ast)$.

---

### Conclusion and final answer

Part 1 shows every solution of $(\ast)$ is of the form $f(x)=x+c$ for some constant $c\ge0$;
Part 2 shows every such function is indeed a solution. Therefore:

$$
\boxed{\text{The solutions are exactly } f(x) = x+c \text{ for } x \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}, \text{ for an arbitrary constant } c \ge 0.}
$$

**Verification of the answer.** For $c \ge 0$ and any $x,y \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}$: with
$f(x)=x+c$, $f(y)=y+c$, direct substitution gives $f(x)+y = x+y+c = x+f(y)$, so the middle
quantity in $(\ast)$ is $(x+y+c)/2$, and (using the QM–AM–GM computation above with
$a=x,\,b=y+c$)
$$
\sqrt{\frac{x^2+(y+c)^2}{2}} \;\ge\; \frac{x+(y+c)}{2} \;\ge\; \sqrt{x(y+c)},
$$
with equality throughout iff $x=y+c$ (the equality case of $(a-b)^2\ge0$) — confirming both
that the family satisfies $(\ast)$ for every $c\ge0$, and pinpointing exactly when the chain
degenerates to equality (namely $x=f(y)$), matching the mechanism used in Step 1 of Part 1.
This closes the loop: the special substitution $x=f(y)$ used to derive (FE) is precisely the
equality case of the inequality just verified.

$\blacksquare$

**Cheap-kill / pruning notes:**
- `x=f(y)` substitution is the single highest-value cheap move (one line,
  yields the exact functional equation `f(f(y))=2f(y)-y`, plus injectivity for
  free). Do this first.
- The orbit-positivity argument (`f(x) ≥ x` globally) is another cheap,
  fully rigorous consequence — worth stating as a lemma before attacking the
  harder "d is globally constant" step.
- Symmetric substitution `y = f(x)` was checked and gives **no new
  information** — it only reproduces the trivial identities `2(x-F)^2 ≥ 0` and
  `(F-x)^2 ≥ 0` (where `F=f(x)`), already implied by the `f(f(y))=2f(y)-y`
  lemma. Do not spend outline budget re-deriving from this substitution.
- Setting `x=y` also gives no new information: the middle term becomes exactly
  `AM(x,f(x))`, so the chain is just the trivial QM-AM-GM fact for the pair
  `(x,f(x))`. Confirmed by direct computation — dead end, skip.
