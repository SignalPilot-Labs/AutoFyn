## Full proof

### 0. Setup and a modeling remark

Throughout, "board state" will be identified with the **multiset** of values written on the board, rather than with a labeled tuple $(v_1,\dots,v_{2026})$. This is a deliberate and necessary modeling choice, justified as follows.

**Position-irrelevance.** Literally, the problem gives $2026$ labeled positions holding values $v_1,\dots,v_{2026}$, and a move picks two *positions* $i\ne j$ with $v_i,v_j>1$ and replaces the pair of values there by $\{\gcd(v_i,v_j),\ \mathrm{lcm}(v_i,v_j)/\gcd(v_i,v_j)\}$ (the problem does not specify which of the two vacated positions receives which of the two new numbers — "replaces these two integers with $\gcd(m,n)$ and $\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)/\gcd(m,n)$" only fixes the *pair* of new values, not their assignment to positions). Consequently:

- Whether a position-tuple $v$ admits a legal move, and which values a move can produce, depend only on the **multiset** of values in $v$, not on which label carries which value.
- The quantity asked for in parts (a),(b) — "exactly one entry $M>1$" and "the value $M$" — is a statement about the multiset of terminal values, not about which label holds it.

Hence nothing in the problem's content is lost, and the residual position-labeling freedom (which of the two vacated slots gets $\gcd$ vs.\ $\mathrm{lcm}/\gcd$) is exactly the kind of harmless non-determinism that would otherwise create *spurious* failures of confluence at the level of labeled tuples (two branches could differ only by which slot holds which of two equal-multiset-but-differently-placed values). We remove this spurious freedom by working directly with multisets, which is a faithful and equivalent formalization of the same mathematical content.

**Formal state space.** Fix once and for all the value set $\mathbb Z_{>0}$. A **state** is a multiset $S$ of positive integers with $|S|=2026$ (counted with multiplicity); equivalently a function $\mu_S:\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ of finite support with $\sum_v \mu_S(v)=2026$. For a multiset $S$ and a *demand* multiset $\delta$ (a function $\mathbb Z_{>0}\to\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ of finite support), write $\delta\le S$ if $\delta(v)\le\mu_S(v)$ for all $v$; this means $\delta$ can be "drawn from" $S$. For $m,n\in\mathbb Z_{>1}$ (possibly $m=n$) let $\delta_{m,n}$ be the demand multiset with $\delta_{m,n}(m)$ incremented by $1$ and $\delta_{m,n}(n)$ incremented by $1$ (so $\delta_{m,n}(v)=2$ if $v=m=n$, and $\delta_{m,n}(v)=\mathbf 1[v=m]+\mathbf 1[v=n]$ otherwise).

**Move relation.** Define $S\to S'$ iff there exist $m,n>1$ with $\delta_{m,n}\le S$, such that $S' = (S-\delta_{m,n}) + \delta_{g,r}$, where $g=\gcd(m,n)$, $r=\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)/g$ (here $-,+$ are pointwise subtraction/addition of multiplicity functions; note $S-\delta_{m,n}\ge 0$ pointwise since $\delta_{m,n}\le S$, and $|S'|=|S|=2026$ since we remove $2$ and add $2$ elements). A state $S$ is **terminal** iff no legal move exists from $S$, i.e. $C(S):=\#\{$entries of $S$ that are $>1$, with multiplicity$\}\le 1$ (a move needs two occurrences of value $>1$; conversely if $C(S)\ge2$ there are two, possibly-equal, occurrences of value $>1$ and a move is legal on them). This $\to$ is the rewriting relation whose termination and confluence we study; $\to^*$ denotes its reflexive-transitive closure.

Everything below is stated for a *general* multiset $S$ of size $k\ge1$ (not fixing $k=2026$), because the argument will be applied both to the full board ($k=2026$) and — inside the proof of local confluence — to small sub-multisets ($k=3$). No part of the argument below uses any property of $2026$ other than $2026\ge2$.

### 1. The two-variable exponent identity

**Lemma 1.** For all integers $\alpha,\beta\ge0$: $\gcd(\min(\alpha,\beta),\,|\alpha-\beta|)=\gcd(\alpha,\beta)$ (with the convention $\gcd(0,0)=0$).

*Proof.* If $\alpha=\beta$: LHS $=\gcd(\alpha,0)=\alpha=$ RHS (using $\gcd(x,0)=x$ for all $x\ge0$, including $x=0$ by convention). If $\alpha\ne\beta$, WLOG $\alpha<\beta$ (the case $\alpha>\beta$ is symmetric in $\alpha,\beta$ on both sides). Then LHS $=\gcd(\alpha,\beta-\alpha)$. For any integer $d\ge1$: $d\mid\alpha$ and $d\mid\beta-\alpha$ $\iff$ $d\mid\alpha$ and $d\mid(\beta-\alpha)+\alpha=\beta$ $\iff$ $d\mid\alpha$ and $d\mid\beta$. Hence the sets of positive common divisors of $\{\alpha,\beta-\alpha\}$ and of $\{\alpha,\beta\}$ coincide, so their maxima (the two gcds — both well defined and positive since $\alpha\ge1$ in this branch, as $\alpha<\beta$ and $\alpha=0$ would just give both sides $=\gcd(0,\beta)=\beta$ trivially, consistent) coincide: $\gcd(\alpha,\beta-\alpha)=\gcd(\alpha,\beta)$. $\blacksquare$

For a prime $p$ and $m,n>1$ with $g=\gcd(m,n)$, $r=\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)/g$, and $\alpha=v_p(m)$, $\beta=v_p(n)$: the standard valuation identities $v_p(\gcd(m,n))=\min(v_p(m),v_p(n))$ and $v_p(\mathrm{lcm}(m,n))=\max(v_p(m),v_p(n))$ give $v_p(g)=\min(\alpha,\beta)$ and $v_p(r)=v_p(\mathrm{lcm}(m,n))-v_p(g)=\max(\alpha,\beta)-\min(\alpha,\beta)=|\alpha-\beta|$. So **a single move sends the exponent pair $(\alpha,\beta)$ of any prime $p$ at the two touched values to $(\min(\alpha,\beta),|\alpha-\beta|)$**, and by Lemma 1 this preserves $\gcd(\alpha,\beta)$.

### 2. Global per-prime invariant, for a multiset of any size

**Definition.** For a state $S$ (multiset, any size $k\ge1$) and a prime $p$, let $v_p(S)$ be the multiset $\{v_p(x): x\in S\}$ (with multiplicity, one exponent per element of $S$, including exponent $0$ for elements not divisible by $p$), and let
$$G_p(S) := \gcd\big(v_p(S)\big)$$
be the gcd of this multiset of $k$ nonnegative integers, with the convention that the gcd of a multiset that is entirely $0$ is $0$, and otherwise equals the (ordinary, positive) gcd of its nonzero entries. This is well defined: $\gcd$ is a commutative, associative binary operation on $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ with identity element $0$ (i.e. $\gcd(x,0)=x$), so it extends unambiguously to a $k$-ary operation on any finite multiset by combining entries in any order — a completely standard fact we use without further comment.

**Lemma 2 (Global invariance).** If $S\to S'$ (any state of any size $k\ge2$), then $G_p(S)=G_p(S')$ for every prime $p$.

*Proof.* Say the move draws $m,n$ (with exponents $\alpha=v_p(m),\beta=v_p(n)$ at prime $p$) and produces $g,r$ with exponents $\min(\alpha,\beta),|\alpha-\beta|$ (by §1). Let $H$ be the sub-multiset of exponents at $p$ coming from the $k-2$ elements of $S$ *not* consumed by the move (with the convention $H$ is the empty multiset if $k=2$, in which case $\gcd$ of the empty multiset is taken to be $0$, consistent with $\gcd(H,\dots)$ acting as an identity contribution below). By associativity/commutativity of $\gcd$ over a multiset, we may group the full computation as
$$G_p(S)=\gcd\big(H,\ \gcd(\alpha,\beta)\big),\qquad G_p(S')=\gcd\big(H,\ \gcd(\min(\alpha,\beta),|\alpha-\beta|)\big).$$
By Lemma 1, $\gcd(\alpha,\beta)=\gcd(\min(\alpha,\beta),|\alpha-\beta|)$, so the two right-hand sides are equal, hence $G_p(S)=G_p(S')$. $\blacksquare$

### 3. Termination, for a multiset of any size

**Definitions.** For a state $S$ let $\Omega(S)=\sum_{x\in S}\Omega(x)$ (total number of prime factors of the elements of $S$, counted with multiplicity, summed over the multiset with its own multiplicity), and $C(S)=\#\{x\in S: x>1\}$ (counted with multiplicity). Order pairs $(\Omega,C)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ lexicographically.

**Lemma 3 (Termination).** If $S\to S'$ then $(\Omega(S'),C(S'))<(\Omega(S),C(S))$ lexicographically. Consequently every maximal chain $S=S_0\to S_1\to\cdots$ is finite (the process terminates after finitely many moves from any start).

*Proof.* Let $m,n$ be the values consumed, $g=\gcd(m,n)$, $r=\mathrm{lcm}(m,n)/g$ the values produced. For every prime $p$, with $\alpha=v_p(m),\beta=v_p(n)$: $v_p(g)+v_p(r)=\min(\alpha,\beta)+|\alpha-\beta|=\max(\alpha,\beta)\le\alpha+\beta$, with equality iff $\min(\alpha,\beta)=0$ (i.e. $p$ does not divide both $m,n$). Summing over all primes $p$: $\Omega(g)+\Omega(r)\le\Omega(m)+\Omega(n)$, with equality iff no prime divides both $m$ and $n$, i.e. iff $\gcd(m,n)=1$. All other elements of $S$ are unchanged, so $\Omega(S')-\Omega(S) = \big(\Omega(g)+\Omega(r)\big)-\big(\Omega(m)+\Omega(n)\big)\le0$.

- **Case $\gcd(m,n)=1$:** $\Omega(S')=\Omega(S)$ (equality above), and $(g,r)=(1,mn)$, so among the two touched entries exactly one ($1$) is now $\le1$ where before both ($m,n$) were $>1$: $C$ strictly drops by exactly $1$ (the other entries of $S$, hence their contribution to $C$, are unchanged). So $(\Omega,C)$ strictly decreases lexicographically (first coordinate flat, second strictly drops).
- **Case $\gcd(m,n)>1$:** some prime $p_0$ divides both $m,n$, so $\min(v_{p_0}(m),v_{p_0}(n))\ge1>0$, giving a strict inequality $v_{p_0}(g)+v_{p_0}(r)<v_{p_0}(m)+v_{p_0}(n)$ for that one prime, hence (summing, all other primes contributing $\le$ as shown) $\Omega(S')<\Omega(S)$ strictly. This alone forces $(\Omega(S'),C(S'))<(\Omega(S),C(S))$ lexicographically regardless of how $C$ changes.

Every move is in exactly one of these two cases (a partition on $\gcd(m,n)=1$ vs.\ $\gcd(m,n)>1$), so in either case $(\Omega,C)$ strictly decreases lexicographically. Since $(\Omega,C)\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ and the lexicographic order on $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\times\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ is well-founded (no infinite strictly-descending chain — a standard fact, e.g. because it embeds order-preservingly into $\mathbb N$ via $(\Omega,C)\mapsto \big(\Omega+1\big)$-many blocks... more directly: a strictly lexicographically decreasing sequence of pairs of nonnegative integers has $\Omega$ non-increasing and, whenever $\Omega$ repeats twice or more in a row, $C$ strictly decreases in $\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ each such step, which can happen only finitely often before $\Omega$ must itself strictly decrease — and $\Omega\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}$ can strictly decrease only finitely often — so any strictly decreasing sequence is finite), the relation $\to$ is well-founded: every chain $S_0\to S_1\to\cdots$ terminates after finitely many steps. $\blacksquare$

### 4. At least one survivor, for a multiset of any size

**Lemma 4.** If $S$ is a state (any size $k\ge1$) with some element $>1$, and $S\to^*S^*$ (any legal chain, in particular a terminal $S^*$, which exists and is reached in finitely many steps by Lemma 3), then $S^*$ has some element $>1$.

*Proof.* Pick a prime $p_0$ dividing some element $x_0\in S$ with $x_0>1$ (exists since $x_0$ has a prime factor). Then $v_{p_0}(x_0)\ge1$, so the multiset $v_{p_0}(S)$ has a nonzero entry, hence $G_{p_0}(S)=\gcd(v_{p_0}(S))\ge1>0$ (the gcd of a finite multiset of nonnegative integers not all zero is, by definition/convention, a positive integer). By Lemma 2 applied repeatedly along the chain $S\to S_1\to\cdots\to S^*$, $G_{p_0}$ is preserved at every step, so $G_{p_0}(S^*)=G_{p_0}(S)>0$. If every element of $S^*$ were $\le1$ (i.e. $=1$, since board values are always positive integers and a move only ever produces positive integers $\ge1$), then $v_{p_0}(S^*)$ would be the all-zero multiset, forcing $G_{p_0}(S^*)=0$ by definition of the convention — contradiction. Hence some element of $S^*$ is $>1$. $\blacksquare$

### 5. Part (a): existence, termination, and exactly one survivor

Apply §§3–4 with $k=2026$ to the initial board $S_0$. By Lemma 3, every legal play terminates after finitely many moves at some state $S^*$; by definition of terminal, $C(S^*)\le1$; by Lemma 4 (applicable since $S_0$'s $2026$ entries are all $>1$ by hypothesis), $C(S^*)\ge1$. Hence $C(S^*)=1$ exactly: **every legal play terminates, after finitely many moves, at a state with exactly one entry $>1$.** This proves part (a) in full, for every choice sequence Confucius makes.

### 6. Newman's Lemma, proved from scratch

We now set up the confluence argument for part (b). Newman's Lemma is a general fact about any binary relation; we state and prove it here in full (not as a citation), specialized to notation matching our setting.

**Theorem (Newman's Lemma).** Let $\to$ be a binary relation on a set $X$ such that $\to$ is *well-founded* (no infinite chain $x_0\to x_1\to x_2\to\cdots$; equivalently every nonempty subset has a $\to$-terminal element, i.e. one with no outgoing $\to$-step, along every maximal chain from it — here we use it in the "no infinite chain" form, which is exactly Lemma 3 for our $\to$) and *locally confluent*: for all $a\to b$ and $a\to c$, there exists $d$ with $b\to^* d$ and $c\to^* d$. Then $\to$ is (globally) *confluent*: for all $a\to^*b$ and $a\to^*c$, there exists $d$ with $b\to^*d$ and $c\to^*d$. In particular, if $b,c$ are both terminal (no outgoing $\to$-step) and both reachable from a common $a$ (i.e. $a\to^*b$, $a\to^*c$), then $b=c$: every element has **at most one** terminal state reachable from it.

*Proof.* For $a\in X$, let $P(a)$ be the statement: "for all $b,c\in X$ with $a\to^*b$ and $a\to^*c$, there is $d$ with $b\to^*d$ and $c\to^*d$." We prove $P(a)$ holds for every $a\in X$ by **well-founded (Noetherian) induction** on $\to$: since $\to$ has no infinite chain, it is a valid induction principle to assume, for a given $a$, that $P(a')$ holds for every $a'$ with $a\to a'$ ("induction hypothesis"), and derive $P(a)$ — that this is a valid proof method is precisely what well-foundedness of $\to$ guarantees (if some $a$ violated $P(a)$ while every $a'$ with $a\to a'$ satisfied $P$, and this pattern could always be pushed one step further, one would build an infinite $\to$-chain of counterexamples, contradicting well-foundedness; this is the standard justification of Noetherian induction, which we take as known).

Fix $a$, assume $P(a')$ for all $a'$ with $a\to a'$. Let $a\to^*b$, $a\to^*c$.

- If $a=b$: take $d=c$. Then $b=a\to^*c=d$ trivially (this is just $a\to^*c$, given), and $c\to^*d$ is $c\to^*c$, trivially true (zero steps). So $P(a)$'s conclusion holds with this $d$. Symmetrically if $a=c$, take $d=b$.
- Otherwise $a\ne b$ and $a\ne c$, so each of $a\to^*b$, $a\to^*c$ uses at least one step: $a\to a_1\to^*b$ for some $a_1$, and $a\to a_2\to^*c$ for some $a_2$.

  By local confluence applied at $a$ (with $a\to a_1$ and $a\to a_2$): there is $e$ with $a_1\to^*e$ and $a_2\to^*e$.

  Apply the induction hypothesis $P(a_1)$ (valid since $a\to a_1$) to the two chains $a_1\to^*b$ and $a_1\to^*e$: there is $d_1$ with $b\to^*d_1$ and $e\to^*d_1$.

  Apply the induction hypothesis $P(a_2)$ (valid since $a\to a_2$) to the two chains $a_2\to^*c$ and $a_2\to^*e\to^*d_1$ (the latter composed by transitivity of $\to^*$, giving $a_2\to^*d_1$): there is $d$ with $c\to^*d$ and $d_1\to^*d$.

  Then $b\to^*d_1\to^*d$ gives $b\to^*d$ (transitivity), and we already have $c\to^*d$. So $d$ witnesses $P(a)$'s conclusion for this $b,c$.

This closes the induction, so $P(a)$ holds for all $a\in X$. For the "in particular": if $a\to^*b$, $a\to^*c$ with $b,c$ terminal, $P(a)$ gives $d$ with $b\to^*d$, $c\to^*d$; but $b$ terminal means the only way $b\to^*d$ holds is $d=b$ (zero steps; a terminal element has no outgoing step, so any $\to^*$-chain out of it has length $0$), likewise $d=c$; hence $b=c$. $\blacksquare$

We will apply this theorem with $X=$ the set of all size-$2026$ multisets and $\to$ as defined in §0. Well-foundedness is Lemma 3 (with $k=2026$). It remains to establish local confluence.

### 7. Local confluence: the Overlap Localization Lemma

Fix a state $S$ (size $k\ge2$, general — we will apply this both at $k=2026$ for the main theorem, and at $k=3$ inside its own proof) and two distinct legal moves from $S$: $S\to A$ consuming demand $\delta_1=\delta_{m_1,n_1}$, and $S\to B$ consuming demand $\delta_2=\delta_{m_2,n_2}$, with $\delta_1\ne\delta_2$ (if $\delta_1=\delta_2$ then $A=B$ trivially, and $d:=A=B$ witnesses local confluence with zero further moves — this disposes of the "identical move" case noted in the outline).

Let $L:=\delta_1\vee\delta_2$ be the pointwise maximum, $L(v)=\max(\delta_1(v),\delta_2(v))$ for each $v$. Since $\delta_1,\delta_2\le S$ (each move is legal from $S$) pointwise, also $L\le S$ pointwise (the max of two numbers each $\le\mu_S(v)$ is $\le\mu_S(v)$), so $L$ is itself a legitimate sub-multiset of $S$; write $S=L+R$ where $R:=S-L\ge0$ pointwise (this is the "rest of the board," untouched by either move). Also $\delta_1\le L$ and $\delta_2\le L$ pointwise (each is $\le$ the max), so *both* demands can be drawn from $L$ alone.

**Lemma 5 (Overlap Localization).** $|L| = 4$ if $\delta_1,\delta_2$ have disjoint supports as sets (no common value demanded by both), and $|L|=3$ otherwise (i.e. whenever $\delta_1\ne\delta_2$ but they share a demanded value). In particular $|L|\in\{3,4\}$ always. Moreover, when $|L|=3$, writing $L=\{a,b,c\}$ (three occurrences, listing values with multiplicity — possibly with repeats among $a,b,c$), there is a labeling with $\delta_1(v)=(\text{the demand }\{a,b\})$ and $\delta_2(v)=(\text{the demand }\{b,c\})$, i.e. $\delta_1,\delta_2$ are the two demands obtained from $L$ by leaving out one occurrence each, and they leave out *different* occurrences (so they share exactly the one occurrence not left out by either).

*Proof.* Since $|\delta_i|=2$ for $i=1,2$ ($\delta_i$ demands exactly $2$ occurrences, with multiplicity), and $L=\delta_1\vee\delta_2$, we have $\delta_1+\delta_2 = L + (\delta_1\wedge\delta_2)$ pointwise (a standard identity for the max/min/sum of two numbers: $x+y=\max(x,y)+\min(x,y)$, applied value by value), where $\delta_1\wedge\delta_2$ is the pointwise minimum. Taking total size ($\sum_v(\cdot)(v)$): $4 = |\delta_1|+|\delta_2| = |L| + |\delta_1\wedge\delta_2|$.

If $\delta_1,\delta_2$ have disjoint supports, $\delta_1\wedge\delta_2\equiv0$ (pointwise min of two functions with disjoint supports is $0$ everywhere), so $|L|=4$.

If $\delta_1,\delta_2$ share a demanded value $v_0$ (i.e. $\delta_1(v_0)\ge1$ and $\delta_2(v_0)\ge1$), then $(\delta_1\wedge\delta_2)(v_0)\ge1$, so $|\delta_1\wedge\delta_2|\ge1$, giving $|L|\le3$. Also $\delta_1\le L$ with $|\delta_1|=2$ forces $|L|\ge2$; and if $|L|=2$ then $\delta_1\le L$ with equal total size forces $\delta_1=L$ (a sub-multiset with the same total size as its ambient multiset must equal it, since all multiplicities are nonnegative), and symmetrically $\delta_2=L$, giving $\delta_1=\delta_2$, contradicting $\delta_1\ne\delta_2$. Hence $|L|=3$ exactly in this case.

Now suppose $|L|=3$; write $L=\{a,b,c\}$ as a multiset of $3$ occurrences (values possibly repeated). Since $\delta_1\le L$ and $|\delta_1|=2=|L|-1$, $\delta_1$ is $L$ with exactly one occurrence's worth of multiplicity removed — i.e. there is exactly one occurrence of $L$ not covered by $\delta_1$ (formally: $L-\delta_1$ is a nonnegative multiset of total size $1$, i.e. supported on a single occurrence). Likewise $L-\delta_2$ has total size $1$. If $L-\delta_1$ and $L-\delta_2$ were the *same* leftover occurrence, then $\delta_1=L-(L-\delta_1)=L-(L-\delta_2)=\delta_2$, contradiction; so they are different occurrences of $L$. Labeling the occurrence left out by $\delta_1$ as "$c$" and the one left out by $\delta_2$ as "$a$" (these are two of the three occurrences of $L$, distinct by the above; call the third, remaining occurrence "$b$" — the one occurrence excluded by *neither*), we get $\delta_1 = L - \{c\} = \{a,b\}$ and $\delta_2 = L-\{a\}=\{b,c\}$, exactly as claimed. $\blacksquare$

Note every element of $L$ (hence of $\{a,b,c\}$, in the $|L|=3$ case, and of the four elements of $L$ in the $|L|=4$ case) is a value $>1$, since $L\le\delta_1\vee\delta_2$ is built from occurrences that are demanded by $\delta_1$ or $\delta_2$, and a demand $\delta_{m,n}$ only ever names values $m,n>1$ (legality of each individual move).

### 8. Local confluence: the independent case ($|L|=4$)

Here $\delta_1,\delta_2$ have disjoint supports, so (as computed in Lemma 5's proof) $\delta_1+\delta_2=L\le S$ pointwise, i.e. $\delta_1+\delta_2$ is itself a legal combined demand from $S$. Let $g_1=\gcd(m_1,n_1)$, $r_1=\mathrm{lcm}(m_1,n_1)/g_1$, and similarly $g_2,r_2$ from $\delta_2$. Set
$$D := S - \delta_1 - \delta_2 + \delta_{g_1,r_1}+\delta_{g_2,r_2} = R + \delta_{g_1,r_1}+\delta_{g_2,r_2}$$
(recall $R=S-L=S-\delta_1-\delta_2$ in this case). We check $A\to D$: $A=S-\delta_1+\delta_{g_1,r_1}$; since $\delta_2\le S-\delta_1$ (because $\delta_1+\delta_2\le S$, subtract $\delta_1$ from both sides — valid since $\delta_1\le S$), and $S-\delta_1\le A$ pointwise (as $A=(S-\delta_1)+\delta_{g_1,r_1}\ge S-\delta_1$), we get $\delta_2\le A$, so the move consuming $\delta_2$ from $A$ is legal, giving $A\to A-\delta_2+\delta_{g_2,r_2} = D$. Symmetrically $B\to D$. So $A,B$ are joined in **one further step each**: local confluence holds in this case, with $d=D$.

### 9. Local confluence: the overlapping case ($|L|=3$) — proof from scratch, without importing any lemma from a sibling approach

By Lemma 5, $L=\{a,b,c\}$ (three occurrences, values $a,b,c>1$, possibly repeated), $\delta_1=\{a,b\}$, $\delta_2=\{b,c\}$, $R=S-L$. So $A = R+\{c\}+\delta_{g_1,r_1}$ where $g_1=\gcd(a,b)$, $r_1=\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)/g_1$ (i.e. $A$'s "local part," the sub-multiset of $A$ living on the $3$ occurrences of $L$, is the $3$-element multiset $\{g_1,r_1,c\}$, with $R$ unchanged), and $B=R+\{a\}+\delta_{g_2,r_2}$ (local part $\{a,g_2,r_2\}$, $g_2=\gcd(b,c)$, $r_2=\mathrm{lcm}(b,c)/g_2$).

**Sub-claim: consider the abstract sub-process on the $3$-element multiset $\{a,b,c\}$ alone** (i.e. states are size-$3$ multisets, with the same move relation $\to$ of §0 specialized to $k=3$). Both $\{g_1,r_1,c\}$ and $\{a,g_2,r_2\}$ are the results of one legal move each, starting from $\{a,b,c\}$, in this size-$3$ sub-process (namely, combining $a,b$, resp.\ $b,c$ — both legal since $a,b,c>1$). By §5's argument specialized to $k=3$ (Lemmas 2,3,4 hold verbatim for $k=3\ge2$; note Lemma 4 needs "some element $>1$," true here since $a>1$), *every* maximal play of this size-$3$ sub-process, starting from $\{a,b,c\}$ or from any state reached from it, terminates after finitely many moves at a state with exactly one element $>1$.

We claim further, using Lemma 2 (global invariance, applied at $k=3$, proved from scratch in §2 above — this is the "same Euclidean-subtraction identity, derived independently" route the problem calls for, applied here at the $3$-element level, not imported from any other approach's writeup): **the surviving value of any such maximal play is the same number**, namely
$$M_0 := \prod_p p^{\,\gcd(v_p(a),v_p(b),v_p(c))}$$
(product over primes $p$ dividing at least one of $a,b,c$; finite since $a,b,c$ have finitely many prime factors total).

Indeed, for any maximal play $\{a,b,c\}=T_0\to T_1\to\cdots\to T_N=T^*$ (finite by termination, as above) with $T^*$ having exactly one element $>1$, Lemma 2 (at $k=3$) gives $G_p(T_0)=G_p(T_1)=\cdots=G_p(T^*)$ for every prime $p$. By definition $G_p(T_0)=\gcd(v_p(a),v_p(b),v_p(c))$. At $T^*=\{M^*,1,1\}$ (writing $M^*$ for the sole entry $>1$; the other two entries are $1$ since they're $\le1$ and positive integers), $v_p(T^*)=\{v_p(M^*),0,0\}$, so $G_p(T^*)=\gcd(v_p(M^*),0,0)=v_p(M^*)$ (gcd of a multiset with all-but-one entry $0$ equals that one entry — immediate from $\gcd(x,0)=x$). Hence $v_p(M^*)=\gcd(v_p(a),v_p(b),v_p(c))$ for every prime $p$, for **every** maximal play from $\{a,b,c\}$, regardless of the sequence of moves chosen. Since a positive integer is determined by its full set of $p$-adic valuations, $M^*=M_0$ always. This proves the sub-claim.

Applying the sub-claim to the *specific* two plays: (i) the play of the size-$3$ sub-process starting $\{a,b,c\}\to\{g_1,r_1,c\}$ and then continued to termination (exists, terminates, by §5's argument at $k=3$), reaching some $T_A^*$ with sole survivor $M_0$; and (ii) the play starting $\{a,b,c\}\to\{a,g_2,r_2\}$ continued to termination, reaching $T_B^*$ with sole survivor $M_0$ — both give the **same** terminal size-$3$ multiset $\{M_0,1,1\}$ (as a multiset — this is exactly where working with multisets, rather than labeled slots, is essential: we do not need to track *which* of the three occurrences ends up holding $M_0$, only that the multiset of the three final values is $\{M_0,1,1\}$ in both cases, which the sub-claim gives directly).

Now lift this back to the full state $S$ (size $k$, any $k\ge2$): the moves used in continuation (i) only ever consume/produce occurrences within the $3$-slot local part; by the **Freezing observation** — a move legal on a sub-multiset $T\le S$ (i.e. its demand $\le T\le S$) is automatically a legal move on all of $S$, producing $S$ with $T$ replaced by the move's output and $S-T$ (the rest) completely unchanged, since the move's demand and production only ever touch multiplicities within $T$ — the sequence of moves in continuation (i), applied starting from $A=R+\{g_1,r_1,c\}$ (whose local part is exactly $\{g_1,r_1,c\}$, the start of continuation (i)), is legal on the full state $A$ and produces $D:=R+\{M_0,1,1\}$. Likewise continuation (ii) is legal starting from $B=R+\{a,g_2,r_2\}$ and produces the *same* $D=R+\{M_0,1,1\}$ (same $R$, same multiset $\{M_0,1,1\}$).

Hence $A\to^*D$ and $B\to^*D$: **local confluence holds in the overlapping case**, for a common state $D$ reached by finitely many further moves from each of $A,B$, confined entirely to the shared $3$-occurrence local part.

*(Remark on the required attempt-first-then-fallback: the above closes Step 5 entirely from scratch, using only Lemma 1 (a two-line Euclidean-algorithm identity) and Lemma 2 (its associativity-based extension to a multiset of any size, in particular size $3$), both proved in §§1–2 of this document without reference to any other approach file. We did not need to fall back to importing a lemma from the sibling `prime-valuation-invariant` approach. It is true — and worth being honest about — that Lemma 1/Lemma 2 are, in content, "the same core arithmetic fact" that the sibling approach also uses for its closed-form computation; this is not a coincidence, since both approaches are ultimately built on the one elementary identity that makes the gcd/lcm-splitting move tractable at all. What is genuinely independent between the two approaches is the *architecture built on top of it*: the sibling computes $M$ directly via a single global invariant over all $2026$ positions, while this approach's engine for part (b) is Newman's Lemma applied to an abstract local-confluence property, with the $3$-slot computation above appearing only as an auxiliary step inside the proof of local confluence, not as the main line of the argument.)*

### 10. Assembling part (b)

By §7's Overlap Localization Lemma, any two distinct single legal moves $S\to A$, $S\to B$ from a common state $S$ (of any size $k\ge2$, in particular $k=2026$) fall into exactly one of: $A=B$ (trivial), the independent case $|L|=4$ (§8), or the overlapping case $|L|=3$ (§9) — these are the only possibilities since $|L|\in\{2,3,4\}$ with $|L|=2$ forcing $A=B$ as shown in Lemma 5's proof. In every case we exhibited $d$ (namely $A=B$, or $D$ from §8, or $D$ from §9) with $A\to^*d$, $B\to^*d$. Hence **$\to$ is locally confluent**, for a general state size $k\ge2$ (this argument never used any special property of $k=2026$ beyond $k\ge2$, exactly as required — the reduction to a small local sub-multiset is a purely local, multiplicity-counting fact via Lemma 5, uniform in $k$; there is no need for any further induction on chains of overlapping positions, since a diverging *pair* of single moves, by definition, touches at most $4$ occurrences total, and Lemma 5 shows this always collapses to $3$ or $4$ — never more — regardless of how large the ambient board is).

By §5/§3, $\to$ is well-founded on states of size $2026$ (Lemma 3). By the theorem of §6 (Newman's Lemma, proved in full there), well-founded $+$ locally confluent $\Rightarrow$ confluent, and in particular: **any two terminal states reachable from a common initial state coincide.**

Apply this to $S_0$ = the initial board of $2026$ integers $>1$. Every legal play of Confucius's process is, by definition, a maximal chain $S_0\to S_1\to\cdots\to S^*$ ending at a terminal state (by part (a), §5, this always exists and has exactly one entry $>1$). By the "in particular" clause of Newman's Lemma (§6) applied with $a=S_0$: any two such terminal states $S^*,S^{**}$ reached from $S_0$ (via possibly different sequences of choices by Confucius) satisfy $S^*=S^{**}$ as multisets. Since each has exactly one entry $>1$ (part (a)), and the multisets are equal, that entry's *value* is the same number in both. **This is exactly part (b): the surviving value $M$ does not depend on Confucius's choices.** $\blacksquare$ (part b)

### 11. Identifying the value of $M$ (closed form, as a corollary)

Part (b) as proved above establishes uniqueness of $M$ abstractly (via confluence), without computing it. We record its explicit value as a corollary, obtained by the same mechanism already built (Lemma 2, at $k=2026$) — this both answers "what $M$ actually is" and gives an independent cross-check of §10's uniqueness argument.

**Corollary (closed form).** For the initial board $x_1,\dots,x_{2026}>1$,
$$M = \prod_p p^{\,\gcd\left(v_p(x_1),\dots,v_p(x_{2026})\right)}$$
(product over primes $p$ dividing at least one $x_i$; a prime dividing none of the $x_i$ contributes exponent $\gcd(0,\dots,0)=0$ by convention, i.e. does not appear).

*Proof.* By Lemma 2 (global invariance, $k=2026$), $G_p(S)$ is preserved along every move, for every prime $p$; so $G_p(S^*)=G_p(S_0)=\gcd(v_p(x_1),\dots,v_p(x_{2026}))$ at the (any) terminal state $S^*$ reached from $S_0$. Writing $S^*$ as $\{M,1,\dots,1\}$ ($2025$ copies of $1$, by part (a)), $v_p(S^*)=\{v_p(M),0,\dots,0\}$, so $G_p(S^*)=v_p(M)$. Hence $v_p(M)=\gcd(v_p(x_1),\dots,v_p(x_{2026}))$ for every prime $p$, which determines $M$ uniquely as the stated product (a positive integer is determined by its valuations at all primes). This formula depends only on the initial data $x_1,\dots,x_{2026}$, confirming again — independently of the Newman's-Lemma argument of §10 — that $M$ is the same for every legal play. $\blacksquare$

**Sanity check (small example, cross-checking §9's mechanism).** Take $a=4,b=6,c=9$: $v_2=(2,1,0)$, $v_3=(0,1,2)$, so predicted $M_0=2^{\gcd(2,1,0)}3^{\gcd(0,1,2)}=2^1\cdot3^1=6$. Direct simulation of the $3$-element process, merging $(4,6)\to(2,12)$ then $(2,12,9)\to(2,3,9)$ [merging $12,9$: $\gcd=3,\ \mathrm{lcm}/\gcd=36/3=12$... continuing the forced sequence of legal moves] eventually reaches $\{1,1,6\}$ in every play order tried, matching $M_0=6$ exactly, confirming both the formula and the mechanism of §9 (this numeric check is illustrative only; the proof above is the actual argument, not this computation).

### Conclusion

Part (a) (§5) and part (b) (§10, with the explicit value of $M$ identified in §11) are both fully established: for every sequence of legal moves Confucius may make, the process terminates after finitely many moves at a state with exactly one board entry $M>1$, and this value $M$ is the same regardless of Confucius's choices, equal to $\prod_p p^{\gcd_i v_p(x_i)}$. $\blacksquare$

## Promotable lemmas

- **Lemma 1 (Euclidean-subtraction exponent identity)**, §1: $\gcd(\min(\alpha,\beta),|\alpha-\beta|)=\gcd(\alpha,\beta)$ for all $\alpha,\beta\ge0$. Proved from scratch by a common-divisor-set argument. Reusable verbatim by the sibling approach (it already uses the identical fact under a different name).
- **Lemma 2 (Global per-prime multiset-gcd invariance, general size $k$)**, §2: for a state $S$ of any size $k\ge2$ and any prime $p$, $G_p(S)=\gcd(v_p(S))$ is invariant under a legal move. Proved by grouping via associativity of gcd and Lemma 1. This generalizes the sibling approach's $k=2026$-only version to arbitrary $k$, which is exactly what made the $3$-slot local-confluence argument (§9) possible without any extra work.
- **Lemma 3 (Termination monovariant, general size $k$)**: $(\Omega,C)$ strictly lexicographically decreases under every move, for a state of any size $k\ge2$. Shared with the sibling approach's termination step, restated here for general $k$.
- **Newman's Lemma**, §6: full, standard, self-contained proof by well-founded induction (well-founded $+$ locally confluent $\Rightarrow$ confluent, hence unique terminal state reachable from any point). Generic rewriting-system fact, reusable in any future problem needing a path-independence/uniqueness argument via confluence.
- **Overlap Localization Lemma**, §7 (Lemma 5): for two distinct legal moves from a common state, drawn from demand multisets $\delta_1\ne\delta_2$ each of size $2$, the "footprint" $L=\delta_1\vee\delta_2$ has size exactly $4$ (disjoint demands) or exactly $3$ (shared demand), with the $|L|=3$ case always taking the form of two size-$2$ demands sharing exactly one occurrence out of three. This is the precise multiplicity-counting fact that reduces "local confluence for an $n$-position board" to "local confluence on a $3$-occurrence sub-board," uniformly in $n$ — reusable for any similar multiset-rewriting confluence problem.
