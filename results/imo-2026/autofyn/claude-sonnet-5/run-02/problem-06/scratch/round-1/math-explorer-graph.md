## imo-2026-06

### Setup recap
Greedy sequence: a_1 given (>1); a_{n+1} = least integer > a_n with gcd(a_{n+1}, a_i) > 1 for all i ≤ n. Must show eventual periodicity of the *gap* sequence: a_{n+T} = a_n + L for some fixed T, L, all n.

### Distinct openings (this lens: primes-as-hypergraph)
Encode each term a_i by its **prime signature** σ(a_i) = set of primes dividing a_i. The defining rule says: σ(a_{n+1}) must intersect σ(a_i) for every i ≤ n — i.e. σ(a_{n+1}) is a **hitting set / transversal** for the family {σ(a_1), ..., σ(a_n)} (viewed as a hypergraph on the vertex set = primes, with a hyperedge per earlier term).

1. **Minimal-hyperedge reduction (key structural fact I can establish).** If A ⊆ B are two members of the family and a candidate set hits A, it automatically hits B (a shared prime with A is also in B). So the ENTIRE hitting requirement at step n is captured by the **antichain of inclusion-minimal signatures** among σ(a_1),...,σ(a_n) — call it M_n. This antichain only changes monotonically: once a genuinely minimal signature (like σ(a_1) itself) appears it is a permanent constraint forever (early terms never get removed); a later term can only add a NEW minimal element if its own signature is not a superset of any existing minimal element, and can only ever shrink/replace existing minimal elements by a strict subset of them (never grow M_n's elements). This is a classic "descent in a finite lattice" pattern *if* the ambient set of primes involved is finite.

2. **Reduction to a finite core + residues mod L (the mechanism, verified numerically below).** If one can show that only finitely many primes S = {p_1,...,p_k} are ever "load-bearing" (i.e. beyond some index N_0 the minimal antichain M_n stabilizes to a fixed antichain M ⊆ 2^S), then whether a candidate integer m is "good" (satisfies the hitting condition) depends *only on m mod L*, where L = p_1⋯p_k (an integer is good iff its signature restricted to S — determined by m mod L — hits every set in M). The selection process a_n ↦ a_{n+1} then reduces to a deterministic map on the finite state space Z/LZ: "next good residue after r, cyclically." Such a map on a finite state space is eventually periodic automatically, and in fact (since "successor in cyclic order among a fixed subset of Z/L" is literally a single L-cycle through all T "good" residues) the process, once in the stable regime, visits all T good residues once per period and increases by exactly L every T steps. **This is exactly the T, L the problem asks for** — T = #{good residues mod L}, L = product of the finite core-prime set. This gives a very concrete target for what "T and L" *are*, once the hard step below is done.

3. **The hard step / true crux — prime-core finiteness.** The one fact that must be proved (and that I was NOT able to shortcut) is that only finitely many primes ever become "load-bearing" (appear in some minimal signature of M_n for arbitrarily large n) — equivalently that the greedy process eventually stops needing new primes to satisfy the covering condition. This needs a growth/counting argument: if infinitely many new primes were forced into minimal signatures, one would expect the smallest valid candidate at that step to have to be divisible by an ever-larger prime not yet "amortized" by the existing core, forcing gaps a_{n+1}-a_n to grow without bound along a subsequence (roughly like needing a multiple of a fresh large prime while avoiding many residue obstructions) — this should contradict a counting/density argument showing that the existing finite core (once "enough" primes are in play) already has bounded gaps between good residues (so the greedy algorithm never has to reach past L to find its next term, hence never needs a genuinely new load-bearing prime). This is the gap in every possible approach along this route and is where the real difficulty of the IMO problem lives.

4. **A cheap sanity/degenerate case.** If a_1 is itself a prime p or a prime power, then EVERY later term simply must be divisible by p (since σ(a_1) = {p} is already a singleton minimal set, forcing all future terms to be multiples of p forever); combined with "smallest integer greater than a_n that is a multiple of p" (any multiple of p already automatically shares p with all previous multiples of p), the sequence becomes a_n = a_1 + (n-1)p exactly — T=1, L=p immediately, no transient. This is confirmed by the computed cases a_1 ∈ {2,3,4,5,6,9,21,25,33} below (all give the trivial T=1 arithmetic progression from the start, gap = smallest prime factor of a_1). This is a strict special case; the genuinely hard content is when a_1 has ≥ 2 distinct prime factors (e.g. a_1=15,35,105), forcing a nontrivial transient before periodicity with a larger core.

### Candidate technique(s)
- Hypergraph/covering-system framing: minimal transversal sets, antichains in the divisor/subset lattice, finite-state eventual-periodicity argument (pigeonhole on a finite state space once the core stabilizes).
- The genuinely hard step (prime-core finiteness) likely needs a counting/density or extremal argument: bound gaps in "good" residues once k primes are active, and show this bound beats the cost of recruiting a (k+1)-th prime, so greedy never needs to.
- Possibly also useful: an explicit sieve/inclusion-exclusion count of "good residues mod L" as a function of the antichain M (to characterize T once L, M are known) — routine once the hard step is settled, not itself hard.

### Cheap-kill candidates
- None that kill the whole problem; but the **singleton-signature case** (a_1 prime power) is a free, fully solved special case worth stating explicitly as a base case / sanity check for any general proof.
- **Monotonicity of M_n** (only shrinks/gets replaced by subsets, never grows in the "wrong" direction) is a cheap structural lemma, easy to formalize, and should be part of any approach's early lemmas.
- Parity/pigeonhole on "how many minimal sets of size ≤ k exist" bounds the number of times M_n can change (bounded by the length of a maximal chain of antichain-refinements in 2^S, which is finite once S is finite) — this is the mechanism for eventual constancy of M_n GIVEN finite S, but does not by itself give finiteness of S.

### Small-case / intuition notes (all conjectural, based on direct computation of the greedy sequence)
- a_1 = 2,3,4,5,6,9,21,25,33 (prime or prime-power seeds, or seeds whose smallest prime factor immediately dominates): sequence is IMMEDIATELY the arithmetic progression a_n = a_1 + (n-1)·p where p = smallest prime factor of a_1. T=1, L=p, no transient. (Verified by direct computation.)
- a_1 = 15 (=3·5): sequence transients through 15,18,20,24,30,36,40,42,45,48,50,54,60,... and then the gap sequence becomes exactly periodic with period T=8 and L=30=2·3·5, i.e. a_{n+8}=a_n+30 for n beyond a small transient. The periodic "good" residues mod 30 are exactly {0,6,10,12,15,18,20,24} — precisely the residues divisible by at least 2 of {2,3,5}. (Verified numerically, periodicity checked for hundreds of terms.) a_1 = 225 (=15²) gives the identical periodic tail (T=8, L=30) after a short transient — consistent with the core-prime-set for the "3,5-generated" family being {2,3,5} regardless of exact starting multiple.
- a_1 = 35 (=5·7): longer transient, but gaps become exactly periodic with T=34, L=210=2·3·5·7 (verified by direct simulation and exact-match check over ~5000 terms, no deviation). New "incidental" large primes (11, 13, 17, ... up to 1800+) keep appearing in the factorizations of later terms even in the "stable" periodic regime — but these are irrelevant/incidental: the residue mod 210 alone determines selection, and the extra large-prime factors of e.g. 110=2·5·11 do not affect whether 110 is "good," since 110's status is already decided by its divisibility by 2 and 5 (both in the core {2,3,5,7}). This numerically confirms opening (2)/(3) above: the process tolerates unboundedly many *incidental* primes appearing in factorizations while the *load-bearing* core stabilizes to a small finite set.
- a_1 = 105 (=3·5·7): still has a longer transient in the tested range (200 terms not yet clearly periodic); consistent with a larger or later-stabilizing core, but not contradicting the conjecture — would need a longer run to pin down T, L exactly.
- Working conjecture (unproven): for ANY starting a_1, the "load-bearing" prime core S is finite, and L = ∏_{p ∈ S} p (or a related quantity depending on prime *powers* actually needed — note 225=15² gave the SAME L=30, not 30·(extra power of 3 or 5), suggesting L only depends on the *set* of core primes and not exponents, at least in these examples) while T = number of "good" residues mod L under the stabilized minimal-signature antichain.
