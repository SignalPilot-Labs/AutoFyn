## imo-2026-04
- Distinct openings:
  1. **Angle-simplex minimax recursion.** Normalize angles by dividing by $180^\circ$, so a position is an ordered triple $(a,b,c)>0$ with $a+b+c=1$, and put $t=\theta/180^\circ$. A cut from the vertex of angle $a$, parametrized by $0<x<a$, gives the two possible retained triangles
     \[
     (x,b,1-x-b)=(x,b,a+c-x),\qquad (a-x,c,1-a+x-c)=(a-x,c,b+x).
     \]
     (The other vertices are cyclic permutations.) Thus the game is a continuous AND/OR reachability game: Mulan chooses a vertex and $x$, while Shan-Yu chooses one of these two children. This coordinate rule is the appropriate starting model and is useful for checking every proposed strategy.
  2. **Rational finite-state retrograde analysis.** Restricting all angles and cuts to multiples of $1/N$ gives a finite game on positive integer triples summing to $N$. Mark triples containing target $k$ as rank $0$; iteratively mark a triple if some integer cut has both children already marked, assigning rank $1+\min\max$ of child ranks. This computation strongly conjectures that $k/N$ is globally winning exactly when its reduced numerator is $1$, i.e. $t=1/n$. For every $3\le N\le60$, the winning grid targets were exactly the divisors $k\mid N$ (equivalently $k/N=1/(N/k)$), plus the formally impossible-to-play small-grid initial-terminal cases; no reduced fraction with numerator $>1$ survived.
  3. **Depth/rank opening for sufficiency.** For $t=1/n$, refinements $N=nq,k=q$ were winning for every tested $2\le n\le20$, $1\le q\le12$. Once the grid is sufficiently refined, the worst minimax ranks stabilize independently of $q$. For the coarsest grid $(N,k)=(n,1)$ the maximum ranks for $n\ge6$ change at $n=6,9,15,27,51$; equivalently the observed bound is the least $d$ with $n\le 3\cdot2^d+2$. This suggests a dyadic interval-shrinking or doubling potential, rather than an induction that decreases $n$ by one. The exceptional ranks at very coarse grids come from many positions already containing the target and should not be overinterpreted.
  4. **Residue/coset opening for necessity.** Losing states for reduced $t=p/q$ with $p>1$ display strong periodic residue patterns. Examples in integer units: for $(q,p)=(5,2)$ the losing triples are permutations of $(1,1,3)$; for $(7,2)$ they are exactly positive triples all odd; for $(8,3)$ examples include permutations of $(1,2,5)$ and $(2,2,4)$; for $(15,2)$ the first large family consists of all-odd triples. This points to a counterstrategy expressed in the quotient group $\mathbb R/t\mathbb Z$ (or modulo $p$ after scaling), not merely a finite-grid obstruction. The same language can potentially cover irrational $t$ by choosing an initial residue/coset configuration. One-line idea only: seek a Shan-Yu invariant saying that after any real cut at least one child remains in a target-avoiding residue class.
  5. **Predecessor geometry / set closure.** The one-move conditions can be listed directly from the coordinate formulas: absent an old target at $b,c$, the left child hits $t$ only if $x=t$ or $a+c-x=t$, and the right child hits $t$ only if $a-x=t$ or $b+x=t$. Simultaneously forcing both therefore imposes simple affine relations among $a,b,c,t$. Iterating this suggests studying the backward winning regions as unions of affine hyperplanes in the angle simplex. Experimentally, universal coverage occurs for reciprocal integers and fails for other rationals. This is a distinct route from guessing a single explicit move rule.
