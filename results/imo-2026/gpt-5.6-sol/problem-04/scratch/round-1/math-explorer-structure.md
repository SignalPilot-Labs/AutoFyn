- Distinct openings:
  1. **Angle-triple impartial reachability game.** Normalize by dividing all angles by `180°`; a state is an unordered triple `(a,b,c)>0` with `a+b+c=1`, and the target is `t=θ/180°`. More precisely, for an oriented state `(a,b,c)`, choosing a point on the side opposite the vertex of angle `a` is equivalent to choosing `x` with `0<x<a`; the two possible retained children have angle triples
     `C_1=(x,b,1-b-x)` and `C_2=(a-x,c,b+x)`.
     (Interchanging the two endpoints of the opposite side interchanges `b,c`.) Thus Mulan needs one choice `(vertex,x)` for which **both** children lie in an earlier winning layer. This suggests defining target states `W_0={triples containing t}` and attractor layers `W_{r+1}=W_r ∪ {S: some cut has both children in W_r}`. A whole-solution opening is to identify these layers explicitly for `t=1/n`, with the layer index furnishing a uniform finite-move bound.
  2. **Integer-/ceiling-valued rank attached to multiples of `θ`.** Grid experiments strongly suggest the winning set is exactly `θ=180°/n`, `n≥2`. For these targets, the unexpectedly tiny maximal attractor depth (about `⌊√n⌋` on fine rational grids; see below) points to a coarse Euclidean/interval rank rather than tracking exact angles. Candidate statistics are counts such as `⌊a/t⌋,⌊b/t⌋,⌊c/t⌋`, residues modulo `t`, or the number/maximum size of target-free subintervals among the marks `0,t,2t,…,nt=1`. The key structural fact to exploit is that a cut redistributes one parent angle while preserving two old endpoint angles in complementary children, so a suitable `t`-grid cut can create a two-way threat. Stop at this pointer: no explicit rank has yet been verified.
  3. **Adversarial invariant for necessity via additive cosets.** If `t` is not a unit fraction, Shan-Yu plausibly starts from a carefully chosen equilateral/isosceles triple and always retains a child whose angles avoid `t`; merely avoiding `t` is not enough because Mulan may create it in both children. The transition formulas turn a simultaneous threat into two linear equalities in `x`; hence for a prescribed parent there are only finitely many `x` at which each child contains `t`, and a forced win in one move requires overlap between the two finite menus. A durable obstruction may be a residue/coset coloring modulo the additive subgroup generated by `t` and `1`, maintained by choosing one child. Rational non-unit targets `t=p/q` (`p>1`) should admit a finite residue-state trap; irrational targets should admit an affine-lattice/coset trap. This is a distinct whole route for the losing direction, not yet formalized.
  4. **Symmetric safe-state route.** The equilateral state `(1/3,1/3,1/3)` is a natural adversarial seed. Under a cut at a vertex, Shan-Yu can choose between `(x,1/3,2/3-x)` and `(1/3-x,1/3,1/3+x)`. For many non-unit rational targets, finite-grid retrograde analysis leaves equilateral in the losing kernel (examples: `t=5/12,7/12,8/12,4/15,3/10`). This suggests seeking a symmetry-closed family (isosceles triples, or triples whose coordinates lie in selected residue classes) where every cut has at least one retained child in the family and no coordinate equals `t`.
  5. **Backward interval geometry.** For fixed `t`, the condition that a child contains `t` is a finite set of linear requirements on `x` (`x=t`, `a-x=t`, `b+x=t`, `1-b-x=t`, or an unchanged old angle already equals `t`). Therefore the set of cuts that win immediately can be computed exactly; iterated preimages remain unions of polyhedral regions/line segments in the open triangle simplex. This opens a geometric induction on polyhedral winning layers, potentially exposing why only reciprocal targets cover the whole simplex. It is structurally different from a numeric floor-rank proof.
