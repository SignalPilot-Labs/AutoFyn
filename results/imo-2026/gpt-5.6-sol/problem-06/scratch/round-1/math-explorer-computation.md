## imo-2026-06
- Distinct openings:
  1. **Exact P-position / greedy-set identification.** The crux corpus contains an essentially exact predecessor: in `aimo-0030`, for fixed cutoff `k`, the “good” (losing/P-position) integers are enumerated by starting at `k` and repeatedly taking the least larger integer non-coprime to every previously enumerated good integer. Thus the target sequence with `k=a_1` is exactly that good-number enumeration. The corpus solution establishes that good/bad status depends only on the set of prime divisors at most `k`; consequently the indicator word on integers `m>=k` is periodic modulo the product of primes `p<=k`, and enumeration of its marked positions gives a translation-periodic increasing sequence. This is not a citation-ready proof, but it is an exact analogue whose argument can be adapted from scratch.
  2. **Direct bounded-prime-support classification.** The computationally visible invariant is stronger than mere pairwise non-coprimality: any two selected terms appear to share a prime from a fixed finite set determined by `a_1`. The safe corpus-backed cutoff is all primes `p<=a_1`. If one proves directly that whether an integer is selected depends only on which primes `p<=a_1` divide it, selection becomes a periodic subset of the integers modulo the primorial `P=prod_{p<=a_1}p`; no dynamical state beyond the residue class is needed.
  3. **Finite residue-state formulation.** Encode an integer by the bit mask of primes `p<=a_1` dividing it. The target greedy condition asks that the new mask intersect every earlier selected mask. Once selection/nonselection is shown to be a function of this finite mask (rather than of large prime factors), the infinite greedy process reduces to a finite periodic word. This is a computationally transparent alternative presentation of the same structural phenomenon, useful if the proof is organized without game language.
  4. **Minimal-counterexample descent eliminating large primes.** The exact analogue’s second solution proves that any two good numbers share a “small” prime (`p<=k`) via a smallest violating pair and replacement by a number having the same small-prime mask but no large prime factors. This is the key opening if the shared finite-support statement is attacked directly. Stop at this pointer: the outliner should decide whether to package it through P-positions or directly through the greedy recursion.
- Candidate technique(s): P-position recursion for a terminating impartial game; encoding by finite prime-divisibility masks; periodic indicator sets modulo a primorial; minimal-counterexample descent to remove prime factors above the fixed cutoff; then conversion from a periodic indicator word to translation-periodicity of its increasing enumeration.
- Cheap-kill candidates: First set `k=a_1` and recognize that the recursion already enumerates a fixed subset of `[k,infinity)`; there is no need to track growing gcd values or prime exponents. Pairwise selected terms are automatically non-coprime. The decisive finite pruning is to replace arbitrary prime support by the mask of primes `<=k`. Once that classification is available, periodicity is immediate modulo `P=prod_{p<=k}p`, and one period contains at least one selected residue (multiples of `k` are candidates in the exact game formulation), so the enumeration has a positive count-period `T` and translation `L=P` (or a divisor of `P`).

  - If a membership word `X_m` is periodic with modulus `L` and has exactly `T>0` selected residues per period, then its ordered selected positions satisfy `a_{n+T}=a_n+L`. This conversion is elementary but the positivity/nonemptiness of selected residues must be stated; the exact analogue supplies infinitely many good numbers (e.g. multiples of `k`).
