## imo-2026-06
- Distinct openings:
  1. **Prime-support hypergraph / redundancy route.** Replace each term by its set of prime divisors. The condition `gcd(x,a_i)>1` says that the prime support of `x` is a transversal (hitting set) of all earlier supports. Only inclusion-minimal earlier supports impose nonredundant conditions. The key opening suggested by the corpus is therefore to show that the greedy process eventually (or, more strongly, within its first translation block) has only finitely many essential supports/primes. Once that is known, admissibility is a fixed finite Boolean combination of divisibility conditions and hence is periodic modulo the product/lcm of the essential primes; the increasing sequence is then the ordered enumeration of a periodic set. This is an end-to-end route if one can also account for the stronger quantifier `a_{n+T}=a_n+L` for every `n`, rather than merely an eventual tail.
  2. **Finite-state residue route.** Seek a finite modulus `M` encoding all nonredundant gcd tests, and encode the process by the current residue/position inside one `M`-block together with the finite family of active minimal prime supports. Repetition of the deterministic state would give a translated repeat. This is the closest adaptation of `aimo-0678`, but the hard input here is not boundedness of the values (they grow): it is stabilization or recurrence of the active constraint family. A useful one-line structural fact is `a_{n+1} <= 2a_n`, since `2a_n` has gcd greater than 1 with every earlier term; this gives a dyadic window in which to search for state compression.
  3. **Minimal-support witnesses plus size bound.** If the squarefree support of a new term is inclusion-minimal as a transversal, then every prime in it has a witness among earlier terms that is hit by that prime and by no other selected prime. Attach such witnesses in a prime-incidence table. The `aimo-0447` move suggests bounding how often a prime can service witnesses in a short interval and comparing the forced product of distinct primes with the cheap upper bound `a_{n+1} <= 2a_n`. A contradiction for sufficiently many genuinely new essential constraints would force finiteness/stabilization, after which periodic-set enumeration finishes the claim.
  4. **First-block self-replication / induction route.** Computations indicate that the difference word is periodic from the first term, not merely eventually. Look for a first return `a_{T+1}=a_1+L` in which translation by `L` preserves every gcd-admissibility test and every rejected integer between successive selected terms. This would permit block induction. Likely `L` is the squarefree product of a finite set of essential primes (not the lcm of all terms): examples give `L=30` for `a_1=15`, `L=210` for `35` and `105`, and `L=154` for `77`. This route directly targets the global-from-`n=1` conclusion and avoids the possible weakness of an eventual-periodicity argument.
  5. **Greedy enumeration of a fixed periodic set.** Try to identify, from one finite initial block, a family `F` of prime supports such that every later term is automatically redundant as a gcd constraint. Then the sequence is exactly all integers above `a_1` whose support hits every member of `F`. Any such set is periodic modulo `L=prod_{p in union(F)} p`; ordering its elements immediately produces a periodic gap word and hence a translation law. This is distinct from finite-state recurrence because it aims to prove the state becomes literally fixed, not merely recurrent.

The likely conceptual answer is that the gap sequence `a_{n+1}-a_n` is periodic from its first term, with `L` a squarefree modulus generated by finitely many essential primes.
